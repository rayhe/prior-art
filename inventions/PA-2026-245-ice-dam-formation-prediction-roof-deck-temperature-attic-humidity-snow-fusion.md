## Abstract

Disclosed here is a system that predicts ice dam formation at roof eaves and estimates meltwater intrusion risk before water reaches the attic. Five channels fuse on an edge processor. Roof-deck temperature is measured at the ridge, mid-slope, and eave, giving a temperature gradient along the slope. Attic air temperature and relative humidity give dew point and the risk of condensation on the sheathing. Snow depth and snow water equivalent are measured on the roof. Meltwater presence is sensed at the eave and the wall plate line. A weather-data feed supplies air temperature, snowfall, and rain-on-snow forecasts. Each channel is read continuously through the snow season and trended against a per-house learned baseline. A heat-loss model converts ceiling heat flow into a meltwater production rate in gallons per day. A signature table maps combinations of channel deviations to ten specific failure modes. It distinguishes faults that present identically from the street, such as a bypass air leak versus blocked soffit ventilation, and a sun-driven melt versus a heat-loss melt. It computes a composite Ice Dam Risk Index and issues part-level diagnoses with confidence scores. It controls a heat cable only when meltwater is actually present. And it shares anonymized failure signatures across a fleet to detect bad production batches and regional storm-driven patterns. All thresholds, scores, and projections in this disclosure are reasoned starting points, not measured results: the system has not been built, and none of it has been validated against a fleet of houses. As a retrofit, sensors mount under the eaves and inside the attic, with no change to the roof covering, the structure, or the heating system.

## Technical Field

This disclosure covers diagnostics for residential roofs in cold climates: multi-sensor fusion and edge inference that tell apart the heat-loss, ventilation, moisture, and drainage faults that form ice dams, before meltwater intrudes.

## Background

### How an Ice Dam Forms

An ice dam needs three things at once, and the University of Minnesota Extension states them plainly: snow on the roof, the upper part of the roof surface above 32 degrees Fahrenheit, and the lower part below 32 degrees, with outside air below freezing too. Meltwater runs down the warm upper slope, reaches the cold eave, and freezes. Fed by melting snow above it, the dam grows, and it limits itself to the part of the roof that averages below freezing, so the water above it backs up behind the dam and stays liquid. That pond finds cracks and openings in the roof covering, flows into the attic, and from there into exterior walls or through the ceiling insulation to stain the ceiling. Heat from the house is what warms the upper roof. It travels by conduction through the ceiling and insulation, by convection as warm air leaks through gaps and rises to the roof, and by radiation from the warm top surface of the insulation to the sheathing. Three defects feed it: inadequate insulation, poor air sealing of the ceiling plane, and inadequate attic ventilation. Building Science Corporation puts airtightness first, ahead of insulation, and its classic control is a vented roof: an airtight ceiling lid, heavy insulation on top of it, and ventilation that flushes whatever heat still reaches the deck with cold outside air. Done well, no ice dams. Done badly, which describes most existing houses, the roof melts its own snow and refreezes it at the edge.

It does not take much snow. Building America Solution Center notes that ice dams can form with as little as 2 inches of snow on a roof. They are likely anywhere the ground snow load exceeds 30 pounds per square foot, and prevalent where it exceeds 60, according to Joseph Lstiburek. Snow itself makes the problem worse: a snow blanket insulates at about R-1 to R-2 per inch, so the snow that is melting on top is also trapping the heat that melts it. Valleys, dormers, chimneys, and skylights make it worse again, because every intersection is a place where air sealing, insulation, and ventilation are harder to do well, and where meltwater collects. A simple gable roof with a tight ceiling and clear soffit vents is the roof least likely to dam. A complex roof is a set of dams waiting for a cold week.

A house built to R-19 in the attic, which was common for decades, leaks about three times the heat of a house built to R-60, and every extra unit of heat is meltwater waiting for a cold night. Soffit vents get buried by insulation in house after house, and a vent that is buried is a vent that does not exist.

### The Heat-Loss Math: How Much Meltwater a Ceiling Makes

The melt rate follows directly from the heat flow, and the arithmetic is short enough to check by hand. Heat flow through a ceiling is area times temperature difference divided by R-value. Start with a 1,000 square foot ceiling insulated to R-19, with 70 degrees inside and 20 degrees in the attic, a 50 degree difference. Heat flow is 1,000 times 50 divided by 19, about 2,632 Btu per hour. Over 24 hours that is about 63,200 Btu. Melting ice takes 144 Btu per pound, the latent heat of fusion, so 63,200 Btu melts about 439 pounds of ice a day, which is about 53 gallons of water a day. Not all of that heat reaches the roof deck, and not all of the melt runs to the eave, so call the real figure half that and it is still about 26 gallons a day, every day, for as long as the cold snap lasts. Insulated to R-60 instead of R-19, the same house makes a third as much meltwater from the same temperature difference, about 17 gallons a day before the same halving, or about 8 gallons a day after it, which is the entire argument for insulation stated as a water bill.

Meltwater per day, 1,000-square-foot ceiling, 50 degree indoor-attic difference

| Ceiling | Gross melt | After halving allowance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| R-19 | 53 gallons a day | 26 gallons a day |
| R-60 | 17 gallons a day | 8 gallons a day |

Conduction is only one path. Air leakage is the other, and Lstiburek's point is that it is usually the bigger one: warm air leaking through a single unsealed plumbing stack chase or a set of recessed can lights can carry more heat into an attic than the whole ceiling conducts, and it carries moisture with it.

Moisture is the second half of the problem, and it follows its own arithmetic. Indoor air at 70 degrees and 35 percent relative humidity has a dew point near 40 degrees. Roof sheathing on a cold night sits well below 40 degrees, often near the outdoor temperature, so any house air that reaches the sheathing condenses on it. That condensation freezes, and the frost that builds up on rafters and sheathing melts in the spring, which is when attic mold starts. That same air leak melts snow on the roof and wets the attic from the inside, and a system that only watches the roof misses half the damage. Dew point is computed from attic temperature and humidity, two cheap readings, and the gap between the dew point and the sheathing temperature is a direct measure of how much condensation is happening tonight.

### Snow Weight and Ponding: What the Roof Is Carrying

Snow depth is not the load. Water content is. As a rule of thumb, 10 inches of snow holds 1 inch of water, but real ratios run wide: fresh light snow holds about 8 percent water, settled snow a few days old about 25 percent, wet heavy snow about 40 percent, and packed snow with ice layers about 50 percent. One inch of water spread over one square foot weighs about 5.2 pounds, since a cubic foot of water weighs 62.4 pounds. Twelve inches of settled snow at 25 percent water holds 3 inches of water, which is 15.6 pounds per square foot, and across a 1,200 square foot roof that is about 18,720 pounds, over nine tons. Those same 12 inches as wet heavy snow at 40 percent water weigh 25 pounds per square foot, about 30,000 pounds on the same roof. Ice is heavier still at about 57 pounds per cubic foot. An ice dam 4 inches thick, 12 inches wide, running 40 feet along an eave holds about 13.3 cubic feet of ice, about 760 pounds, sitting exactly where the gutter and the fascia are weakest. None of this is the load the roof was designed for in the usual sense: the dam is a concentrated load at the edge, plus a pond behind it. A pond 1 inch deep over an 80 square foot band behind the dam holds about 50 gallons of water, and that water is sitting on shingles that were designed to shed water running downhill, not to hold a pond.

### What Exists Already

Homeowners are not without tools, and the gap this disclosure fills is specific, not total. Naming what exists is part of the disclosure, because the new contribution sits in the space between these products.

Heat cable is the fix people reach for after the first leak. Constant-wattage kits, such as the EasyHeat ADKS, put out about 5 watts per foot whenever they are plugged in: a 120-foot run draws 600 watts, 14.4 kilowatt-hours a day if left on around the clock, about $1.60 to $2.15 a day at Minnesota electricity rates, or $144 to $194 over a 90-day season. Self-regulating cable, such as WarmlyYours Ice Shield or nVent Raychem IceStop, varies its output with temperature and costs $2 to $5 a foot installed against $1 to $3 for constant-wattage. Professional installation averages about $981, and hardwired controllers with snow and temperature sensors run $900 to $3,900. Heat cable melts a drain path through a dam so trapped water can escape. It does not stop dams from forming, it does not fix the heat loss that causes them, and the Minnesota Center for Energy and Environment recommends against it. Left on all winter, a cable can add roughly $40 to $200 or more a month to the electric bill, depending on cable length and local rates; the 120-foot run priced above runs about $48 to $65 a month. When a cable trips its ground-fault protection, which the National Electrical Code requires on snow and ice melting circuits, it stops working silently, and the dam comes back.

An ice and water shield is the membrane that saves the house when the dam wins. Where the local code designates a history of ice damming, code requires a self-adhering polymer-modified bitumen membrane from the lowest roof edge to a point at least 24 inches inside the exterior wall line, or two cemented layers of underlayment as an alternative, and it seals around nail penetrations so ponded water cannot reach the deck. It is a second line of defense, not a prevention measure, and it only exists if the roof was replaced after the code applied or the roofer installed it. Millions of roofs have felt paper instead, or a membrane that stops short of the wall line, and a pond that reaches past the membrane reaches the attic. A roof rake, a long-handled blade that pulls snow off the lower roof from the ground, works and costs little, and it also risks damaging shingles and gutters, and it requires someone to use it after every storm. Steam removal, which melts the dam with low-pressure steam, costs $400 to $4,000 a visit and is what professionals do when the leak is already active.

Patents cover pieces of this too, and all of them heat. US 6,184,495, granted in 2001, describes a heating apparatus placed between the roof and the outside wall, under the roof surface, that melts snow on the outer edge and is not visible from outside. US 6,215,102, granted to Jones in 2001, describes a heating apparatus for preventing ice dams with an automatic controller and a ground-fault circuit interrupter that detects ground faults in the heating device. US 7,071,446, granted to Bench in 2006, describes a de-icing and snow melting system using heatsink strips with a heating cable in a gap, a low-voltage transformer, and controls with self-diagnostics that detect anomalies. Every one of these patents is a heater with a controller. None of them senses the roof-deck temperature gradient, none measures attic humidity or dew point, none measures snow depth or water equivalent, none estimates meltwater production from heat loss, and none diagnoses which fault is causing the dam. In the Jones patent, the controller interrupts the heater on a ground fault condition. It does not predict anything. Two more filings narrow the gap further. US 6,036,102, granted to Pearson in 2000, pairs ambient and roof temperature sensors with a thermostatic controller that runs a power ventilator to cool the roof when ice dam conditions are present, which is sensing plus control, though it ventilates rather than predicting. Published application US 2022/0243474, from Clardy and colleagues in 2022, goes further: a sensor detects the state of water near a heating element and switches heater power based on it, turning the heater on when it detects an ice dam and off when a snow bridge covers the element. That is meltwater-gated cable control, and it narrows the cable-control claim below to the fusion around it: Clardy gates a heater on water state alone, with no deck gradient, no dew point, no snow water equivalent, no learned baseline, and no failure-mode diagnosis.

Smart home sensors cover the edges of the problem and miss the middle. Leak detectors under sinks and behind washing machines are common, and a few homeowners put one in the attic, but a leak detector in the attic reports water that has already arrived, and by then the insulation is wet. Temperature and humidity sensors for attics exist as standalone gadgets, and some weather stations report roof snow depth, but no product found in this search combines the roof-deck gradient, the attic dew point, the snow water equivalent, and meltwater presence into a single prediction, and no product tells the owner which fault to fix. Clardy comes closest on control, sensing water state at the heater, but it senses one point, not the roof, and it diagnoses nothing. Of existing products, the nearest relative is the hardwired heat cable controller with a snow and temperature sensor, which turns the cable on when it snows and the temperature is near freezing. It reacts to weather. It does not know whether the roof is melting its own snow, whether the attic is wet, or whether the cable it controls is still working.

### The Search

Before writing this disclosure, the existing corpus was checked for overlap. This site's 249 prior disclosures include rooftop snow load estimation, pipe freeze detection by acoustic velocity thermometry, attic hail impact assessment, roof drainage acoustic monitoring, roof shingle granule loss analysis, and heat pump frost accumulation and demand defrost, and none of them covers ice dam formation. Distinguishing facts are the operating physics and the failure economics: an ice dam is a slow, seasonal failure driven by the house itself, in which the roof melts its own snow and refreezes it at the edge, and the cost of a missed diagnosis is a wet attic and a stained ceiling, not a failed machine. No disclosure in that corpus combines roof-deck temperature gradient sensing with attic dew point computation, snow water equivalent measurement, meltwater presence sensing at the eave, and a heat-loss melt model for a residential roof. ## Detailed Description

### 1. System Overview

As a retrofit package, it mounts under the eaves and inside the attic, and it wires into the house without cutting the roof covering or changing the heating system. Five channels feed an edge processor, a low-power microcontroller with a Wi-Fi radio, mounted in the attic in a sealed enclosure. Three thermistors on the roof deck, one near the ridge, one at mid-slope, and one at the eave, read the temperature gradient along the slope. On houses with valleys, a valley temperature probe instruments the valley, and on houses where the sun-driven row matters, a north-slope probe instruments the shaded slope. An attic temperature and humidity sensor reads the air the roof breathes, and a sheathing temperature probe reads the surface that condensation forms on. An ultrasonic snow depth sensor on a mast above the roof, or a pressure-based snow load pad under a representative section, measures snow depth, and the weather feed supplies snowfall totals so the depth reading can be checked against what fell. Conductivity strips at the eave, in the gutter, along the wall plate line, and in the valley where the roof has one, detect liquid water where only ice and snow should be. A weather-data feed supplies air temperature, snowfall, and rain-on-snow forecasts over the radio link.

Deck thermistors are sampled every 5 minutes, the attic sensor every 5 minutes, the snow depth sensor every hour and after each snowfall, and the meltwater strips continuously, logging a meltwater event with a timestamp whenever a strip goes wet. All diagnosis runs on the edge processor, so the system works when the internet is down, and the radio is used for alerts, for fetching weather, and for sharing anonymized signatures with the fleet service. Parts cost for the five channels is about $250 to $400 at retail quantities: three deck thermistors and probes near $45, an attic temperature and humidity sensor near $25, an ultrasonic snow depth sensor near $60, conductivity strips and gutter sensors near $40, a microcontroller board with radio near $25, and a sealed enclosure, cabling, and mounting hardware near $75. The valley probe and strip add about $20 where the roof has valleys, and the north-slope probe about $15 where the sun-driven row matters. Installation is about an afternoon for a competent homeowner, and about three hours billed at a roofer or electrician, with no re-roofing required. Nothing here replaces the ice and water shield, the insulation, the air sealing, or the ventilation. It watches them.

### 2. Roof-Deck Temperature Gradient: The Melt Line

The gradient is the channel that turns a roof from a surface into a diagnostic instrument. One temperature reading on a roof means little. Three readings along the slope give a line, and where that line crosses 32 degrees is the melt line. When the ridge probe reads above 32 and the eave probe reads below 32, with snow on the roof, the dam condition is met: snow is melting up high and refreezing down low. A mid-slope probe locates the melt line between the two, and the melt line moves as the day warms and the night cools. A melt line that sits above the eave for hours each afternoon and drops to the eave each night is a dam in the making. When the ridge probe stays above 32 and the melt line never descends past the ridge, the upper roof keeps melting all day and the whole slope feeds the eave. A melt line that sits at the eave all day means the roof is cold and uniform, which is what a good roof does.

Gradient measurement needs two guards against false readings. First, the probes must read the deck, not the sun. A thermistor taped to a dark shingle in direct sun reads the sun, not the deck. Each probe is shielded under a small white radiation cover. It mounts on the underside of the deck from inside the attic where the roof allows it, or under the shingle at the deck surface with a sun shield where it does not. Second, the gradient must be read as a sustained average, not an instant. Reading each probe as a 60-minute rolling mean removes the noise of a passing cloud, while preserving the slow movement of the melt line across the day. Probes are calibrated once at installation against a known reference, and the calibration is rechecked automatically whenever all three probes read the same temperature on a mild, snow-free night, which happens often enough in the shoulder seasons to keep drift visible.

### 3. Attic Temperature and Humidity: The Dew Point Channel

Attic air temperature and relative humidity give the dew point, and the dew point against the sheathing temperature gives the condensation risk. Indoor air at 70 degrees and 35 percent relative humidity has a dew point near 40 degrees. When the sheathing sits at 25 degrees on a cold night, any house air reaching it condenses, and the condensate freezes. Dew point is computed from the attic sensor every 5 minutes and compared with the sheathing probe. A gap of more than 5 degrees between dew point and sheathing temperature, sustained for more than 6 hours, means condensation is happening tonight, and the system logs the hours of condensation as a running total for the season. That total is the mold risk number: a few dozen hours a season is normal for a tight house, and hundreds of hours means the attic is being wetted from the inside every cold night.

Attic temperature also diagnoses the bypass leaks that melt the roof. A tight attic on a 20 degree night sits a few degrees above outdoor temperature, warmed only by conduction through the ceiling. An attic with a bypass leak, an unsealed plumbing stack chase, a set of recessed can lights, an open attic hatch, runs 10 to 20 degrees above outdoor temperature, and the excess tracks the indoor-outdoor temperature difference, which is the signature of house air entering the attic rather than sun or a warm day. Across the season, the attic-outdoor temperature difference is recorded against the indoor-outdoor difference, and a ratio that stays high across cold nights points at air leakage, while a ratio that falls when the house is set back points at the heating system itself. Ventilation is read from the same channel: an attic with working soffit-to-ridge airflow cools toward outdoor temperature overnight, while an attic with blocked soffits stays warm and humid, and the overnight cooling rate separates the two.

### 4. Snow Depth and Water Equivalent: The Load Channel

Snow depth is measured, and water equivalent is estimated from depth, density, and weather, because depth alone does not say how much water the roof holds or how much meltwater the dam will have to hold back. An ultrasonic sensor on a mast measures depth every hour. Density is estimated from the weather feed: fresh snow falling below 20 degrees is light, about 8 percent water; snow that has sat for days is settled, about 25 percent; snow falling near freezing is wet, about 40 percent; and old snow with ice layers is packed, about 50 percent. Water equivalent is depth times density, and load is water equivalent times 5.2 pounds per square foot per inch of water. Snowfall totals from the weather feed check the estimate, and when the two disagree by more than 30 percent, the system flags the depth sensor for a manual check, because wind drifts snow and a single point measurement can be fooled by a drift.

Heat loss becomes a meltwater production rate in the melt model. Ceiling heat flow is area times the indoor-attic temperature difference divided by the ceiling R-value, using the R-value entered at installation or the default for the house's age band. That heat flow, in Btu per hour, divided by 144 Btu per pound, gives pounds of ice melted per hour, and pounds divided by 8.34 gives gallons per hour. Run twice, the model first uses ceiling conduction alone, and once with an air leakage allowance added, because air leakage is usually the larger heat path and the conduction-only figure understates the melt. The canonical house from the Background derivation, a 1,000-square-foot R-19 ceiling with a 50 degree indoor-attic difference, makes about 53 gallons a day by conduction alone, and the model reports that figure with the leakage allowance alongside it, so the owner sees both the floor and the likely reality. For the signature table, the meltwater forecast is the deciding number: a house making about 53 gallons a day gross with a cold eave is a house at high risk of damming, and a house making 5 gallons a day, the halved figure for an R-60 ceiling with a 30 degree indoor-attic difference, is not, whatever the snow depth says.

### 5. Meltwater Sensing: The Intrusion Channel

Conductivity strips detect liquid water where only ice and snow should be, and they are the channel that catches the leak rather than the conditions for one. At the eave, a strip under the first course of shingles goes wet when meltwater ponds behind the dam. A strip along the wall plate line, inside the attic at the top of the exterior wall, goes wet when ponded water gets under the shingles and past the ice and water shield. A strip in the gutter goes wet when the gutter holds liquid water behind a frozen downspout, and a flow sensor in the downspout confirms whether water is actually leaving the roof. Each strip is a pair of stainless conductors on a flexible backing, read as a resistance that falls from megohms to kilohms when water bridges them, and the strips are specified with a self-test that applies a small voltage once a day and confirms the strip still responds.

False readings are the risk here, and temperature is the guard. A strip that reads wet while the eave probe reads below 28 degrees is almost certainly reading frost or condensation, not liquid water, and the system holds the reading as a watch item until the eave warms above freezing. A strip that reads wet while the eave is above 32 degrees and the melt model says water is being produced is a real event. Most important of the four is the plate line strip: water at the eave is a dam, and water at the plate line is a leak into the house, and the two can be hours apart or the same hour, depending on the membrane. A house whose plate line strip goes wet may have hours, not days, before the ceiling stains, and the alert says so.

### 6. Weather: The Forecast Channel

Weather data arrives over the radio link from a forecast service, or from a small temperature and precipitation sensor mounted on the house. It serves three purposes: the melt forecast, the rain-on-snow warning, and the freeze-thaw count. Forecast air temperature and snowfall set the expected meltwater production for the next 72 hours, so the system can warn on Tuesday about a dam that will form on Friday, while there is still time to rake the lower roof or call a roofer. Rain on snow is the sharpest event in the season. Warm rain falling on a snowpack melts it from above while the eave stays frozen. A rain-on-snow forecast with more than a quarter inch of rain triggers a watch even when the deck gradient looks calm, because the rain supplies meltwater the house did not have to make. Freeze-thaw cycles are counted across the season, and a house that has been through twenty cycles with a warm ridge and a cold eave has had twenty chances to dam, which is why the count is part of the baseline. Expected snow depth for the depth sensor check described above also comes from the forecast, and a forecast that says 8 inches fell while the sensor says 3 means the sensor is drifted or buried, not that the roof is clear.

### 7. Edge Diagnostic: The Signature Table

The diagnostic is a signature table, not a black-box model. Rows are failure modes, columns are channels, and each cell states which way that channel should deviate from the house's own baseline. Scoring counts how many cells match what the house is actually doing, weighted by how distinctive each cell is, and the best-matching rows come back with a confidence score. Three matching channels with high distinctiveness is a diagnosis; one matching channel is a watch item. Nothing is diagnosed on a single channel alone, because every single-channel reading has a benign explanation.

| Ice barrier missing or short ⚠ | Gradient dam pattern | Normal or warm | Melt forecast high | **Plate line wet with small eave pond** |
| Attic frost and moisture | Gradient mild | **Dew point above sheathing for hours** | Melt low | Strips dry |
| Heat cable failed | Eave below 32 with cable on | Normal | Melt forecast high | **Eave strip wet, cable current zero** |
| Sun-driven melt (south slope) | **South slope warm, north cold** | Attic near outdoor | Melt only on sunny days | South eave wet only |
| Rain-on-snow event | Gradient collapses, all above 32 | Humidity spikes | Rain in forecast, melt jumps | **All strips wet at once** |

Two marked rows deserve emphasis because they are the ones that put water in the house. A classic ridge-melt dam shows up first as a gradient, not as water. The pond forms before it intrudes. That pattern gets flagged when the ridge stays above 32 while the eave stays below 32 for more than 6 hours a day on three consecutive snow days, days before the pond reaches the plate line. A missing or short ice barrier shows up as the opposite pattern, a plate line strip going wet while the eave pond is still small, and the system treats that combination as an urgent alert rather than a watch item, because the membrane that was supposed to be the second line of defense is not there, and the water has a clear path to the ceiling.

Time-to-intrusion projection is the output that turns a diagnosis into a decision. Meltwater production comes from the heat-loss model, pond volume from the eave strip and the deck geometry, and the time until the pond reaches the plate line from the melt rate and the distance the water must travel back up the slope. If the plate line strip is already wet, the projection is measured in hours and the alert says so. If the pond behind a dam on a low-slope roof is growing at about 26 gallons a day, the halved melt figure for a 1,000-square-foot R-19 ceiling, the projection is the number of days until the pond reaches the wall line, and the alert names the likely day. After every meltwater event, the projection is recomputed, and it is labeled as an estimate with the melt rate and pond volume it was computed from, so the owner can see the reasoning instead of trusting a number.

### 8. Ice Dam Risk Index

Five channels combine into a single Ice Dam Risk Index from 0 to 100, presented with the channel and signature responsible for each point lost. Starting at 100, the thermal subscore loses points for a ridge-eave gradient that straddles 32 degrees, for a melt line that sits above the eave for hours each day, and for an attic temperature ratio that points at air leakage. Moisture subscore loses points for dew point above sheathing temperature, for condensation hours accumulating across the season, and for attic humidity that stays high overnight. Snow subscore loses points for snow water equivalent above the house's seasonal norm, for meltwater production above the conduction model, and for a freeze-thaw count that keeps climbing. Intrusion subscore loses points when a meltwater strip goes wet, and more points when the plate line strip goes wet. Sensor subscore loses points when the snow depth reading and the weather feed disagree, which indicates a drifted or buried sensor rather than a failing roof.

Each subscore is shown separately. The point deductions in the worked example below are illustrative weights, chosen to show how the index moves, not measured values. An index below 80 triggers a service warning, below 60 triggers an urgent alert, and below 40 triggers a critical alert with a time-to-intrusion estimate. Those 80/60/40 thresholds are unvalidated starting points, and every index is labeled as an estimate. No index replaces manual checks: looking in the attic for frost, checking the soffit vents, and raking the lower roof after a storm. Thresholds are defaults, and the owner can tighten them.

**Ice Dam Risk Index, worked example: 58.** Illustrative weights, starting from 100. Thermal subscore, minus 18: ridge above 32 for 7 hours a day while the eave stays below 32, and the attic runs 14 degrees above outdoor on a 20 degree night, an attic-outdoor ratio of 0.28 against the 50 degree indoor-outdoor difference. Moisture subscore, minus 10: dew point above sheathing temperature for 9 hours a night, 120 condensation hours so far this season. Snow subscore, minus 9: the same 12 inches of settled snow as the Background example, 15.6 pounds per square foot, with the melt model forecasting about 53 gallons a day gross, about 26 after the halving allowance. Intrusion subscore, minus 5: the eave strip went wet yesterday afternoon, and the plate line strip is still dry. Sensor subscore, 0: the depth sensor and the weather feed agree within 10 percent. 100 minus 42 is 58, an urgent alert, and the largest loss is thermal, so the problem is heat loss, not snow load or drainage.

### 9. Baseline Learning: The First Snow Season

Every house is different, and a fixed threshold that fits a 1950s Cape Cod will false-alarm on a new house with R-60 and a sealed ceiling, and miss a dam on a 1920s house with no insulation at all. So the baseline is learned from the house it is installed on. For the first snow season after installation, it records without diagnosing: deck gradient by hour of day, attic-outdoor temperature ratio, dew point against sheathing temperature, snow depth and water equivalent, meltwater production, and condensation hours. One season is long enough to include several snow events, and on a house that sees regular snow it is long enough to collect at least 14 snow days, and short enough that the owner gets diagnoses in the same winter the system is installed. In a mild snow year, a house may not see 14 snow days in one season, so the learning period stretches across seasons until it does. The requirement is at least 14 snow days and at least 3 freeze-thaw cycles in that window.

Baselines are stored as a mean and a standard deviation for each metric, and a deviation is counted only when it exceeds two standard deviations from the mean, which keeps ordinary variation from generating alerts. With only a few dozen snow days in the learning window, the sample spread is itself uncertain, and snow data are skewed rather than normal, so the two-standard-deviation rule will need tuning against fleet data before its false-alarm rate is known.

Baselines are relearned after a known change. When the owner air seals the attic, adds insulation, clears the soffit vents, or replaces the roof, they press a button in the app or on the module, and the system starts a new learning period for the affected channels. When the roof is re-shingled, the system pauses diagnosis for 48 hours, because a new roof has no snow load yet and the deck probes were just disturbed. Seasonal drift is handled by a slow update: the baseline mean moves by 1 percent of the difference between the old mean and the new week's mean each week, which tracks gradual changes like settling insulation without absorbing a sudden failure into the baseline. Drift is bounded on purpose: the baseline moves 1 percent of the gap per week, while the dam flag in §7 trips at a gradient sustained over three consecutive snow days. A leak growing by a fraction of a percent a week could be absorbed into the baseline, and at that rate it would take years to double; anything growing faster outruns the drift and trips the flag.

### 10. Display, Gateway, and Fleet Learning

The owner sees the system in three places. A small display near the thermostat shows the Ice Dam Risk Index, the deck gradient, the snow depth, and the attic dew point. A phone app shows the same, plus the signature table with the matching rows highlighted, the time-to-intrusion projection, and the season trend charts for each channel. An alert goes out by text message and push notification when the index crosses a threshold or when a signature matches with high confidence, and the alert names the suspected fault, not just the symptom: "Bypass air leak near the plumbing stack, ridge melting while the eave stays frozen," rather than "Roof alarm."

Where one is installed, the system also controls the heat cable, and it does so only when meltwater is actually present. A cable controller that runs on a timer wastes electricity all winter, about $1.60 to $2.15 a day for a 120-foot run, and a cable that runs on a snow sensor alone runs whenever it snows, whether the roof is melting or not. This system switches the cable on only when the eave strip is wet and the gradient straddles 32 degrees, and off when the strip dries or the eave rises above freezing, which cuts the run time to the hours that matter. Cable current is monitored, and a cable drawing zero current while commanded on is flagged as a failed cable or a tripped ground fault, which is the heat cable failure mode in the signature table.

Fleet learning is the part that helps houses the system has never seen. Anonymized signatures, with no house identity, no location finer than a town, and no owner information, are shared with a fleet service. When a batch of heat cable from one maker starts failing in its second season across dozens of houses, the service can warn every owner with that cable before theirs fails. When a town's houses all show a gradient spike after the same storm, the service can separate a weather event from a house problem for each house individually. When a roof design shows a characteristic valley dam pattern before intrusion, the service can publish that pattern as a signature update, and every installed system learns to watch for it. Participation is opt-in, and a house that never connects to the radio still gets the full local diagnostic, just without the fleet updates.

### 11. Scoring Rejection Rules

A diagnostic that cries wolf gets ignored, and a roof diagnostic that cries wolf gets uninstalled. Diagnosis is therefore held back in conditions the system knows are not faults:

- **Solar gain:** when the south slope is warm and the north slope is cold on a sunny day, with the attic near outdoor temperature, the melt is sun-driven, not house-driven, and the diagnosis is held back until the sun sets and the gradient is read again.
- **Rain-on-snow:** when warm rain is falling on the snowpack, the gradient collapses and all strips go wet at once, and the system reports the event as weather, not as a house fault.
- **After a storm:** for 24 hours after a snowfall of more than 6 inches, because wind drifts snow and the depth sensor reads the drift, not the roof, and because a fresh snowpack has not yet settled into its density.
- **After re-roofing:** 48 hours, because a new roof has no snow load yet and the deck probes were just disturbed.
- **Vacation setback:** when the house is set back below 55 degrees for more than 48 hours, because the heat-loss model assumes normal occupancy, and a cold house melts less snow for a reason that is not a fault.

A diagnosis held back in these conditions is logged, not discarded, so the owner can see what was held back and why. A pattern of repeated hold-backs is itself reported, because a house that needs its diagnosis held back for solar gain every sunny day has a south slope that dams every sunny day, which is a real problem with a real fix, not a sensor artifact.

## Implementation Notes

Installation follows building and electrical practice throughout. Deck probes mount on the underside of the sheathing from inside the attic where the roof allows it, which avoids penetrating the roof covering, and under the shingle at the deck surface with a sun shield where it does not, sealed with roofing sealant at the penetration. Attic sensor mounts near the ridge, away from the hatch and away from any ductwork, because a sensor next to a leaky duct reads the duct, not the attic. Snow depth sensor mounts on a mast above the ridge, clear of drifts against dormers and chimneys, because a sensor in a drift reads the drift. Meltwater strips adhere to the deck under the first course of shingles at the eave, to the gutter floor, and to the top plate inside the attic, and each strip is tested after installation by wetting it with a spray bottle and confirming the reading. Wiring runs in the attic, clear of the insulation where it would be buried, and the module enclosure is sealed and mounted above the attic floor, because an enclosure that sits in insulation overheats and a cable that runs through insulation gets buried by the next top-up.

Electrical safety matters. A heat cable controller switches a resistive load on a dedicated circuit, and the circuit carries ground-fault equipment protection, as NEC 426.28 requires for fixed outdoor deicing and snow-melting equipment; 30 milliamps is the standard ground-fault equipment protection trip level. That is a different device from a Class A ground-fault circuit interrupter, which trips at 4 to 6 milliamps for personnel protection and is not what the snow-melting rule calls for. A cable that trips its protection stops working silently, which is why the system monitors cable current and flags zero current while commanded on. Sensor package draws under 5 watts, about the same as a night light, so the monitor does not become a load that matters. Sensor batteries, where used instead of wired power, are specified as lithium cells rated to minus 40 degrees, because an alkaline cell in a cold attic dies in January, which is exactly when the system is needed. Roof work carries leak and warranty risk: strips and probes that sit under shingles should be fitted by a roofer, electrical work by an electrician, and the work should not be done on an icy roof. Houses with several bad slopes need several probe sets, which adds about $35 per extra slope for the additional probe and strip, on top of the headline parts cost.

Integration with existing house systems is through Wi-Fi for alerts to the owner's phone, and through the thermostat for occupancy and setback state, so the vacation setback rule in §11 reads the actual setback rather than guessing from temperature. A house with no network still gets the full local diagnostic and a display near the thermostat. That display shows the Ice Dam Risk Index, the deck gradient, the snow depth, and the attic dew point, in plain language, because a number that needs a manual is a number that gets ignored.

## Limitations

This system diagnoses the conditions that form ice dams, not the roof. It cannot detect a leak that does not reach the eave or the plate line, such as a leak at a chimney flashing or a skylight curb, and it cannot detect roof damage that admits water faster than any dam can hold it back. Failed flashing leaks in the rain, with or without snow, and no sensor on the deck changes that. All it can do is report that meltwater is present where it should not be, and report it early.

Of all channels, the snow depth sensor is most vulnerable to wind. A single point measurement on a roof reads the drift at that point, and wind piles snow deeper below ridges and against walls, and scours it thin on exposed slopes. Large disagreements get caught by the weather feed check, and the density estimate is a starting point, not a measurement, but a drifted sensor and a real snow load can mask each other for a time, and the output says so rather than pretending certainty it does not have.

Complex roofs confound the gradient. Many valleys, dormers, and skylights mean many melt lines, not one, and three probes on one slope do not describe the slope behind the dormer. As disclosed, the system instruments the worst slope, the one that has dammed before, and a house with several bad slopes needs several probe sets. That is a cost question, not a physics question, and the disclosure should not pretend one probe set covers a complex roof.

No measurement in this system has been validated against a fleet of real houses, because the system has not been built yet. Thresholds in the claims, the 6-hour gradient rule, the three consecutive snow days, the two-standard-deviation rule, are reasoned starting points from the physics and the published building science, not measured results. A builder would need to instrument a few dozen houses across a season, record the dams that actually occur, and tune the thresholds against those outcomes before the confidence scores mean what they claim to mean.

## Strongest Case Against

Strongest case against this disclosure is that most of it already exists in pieces, and the pieces are close enough that a skeptic could call the combination obvious. Heat cable with a snow and temperature sensor already exists, and the Jones patent already puts a controller on a roof heater with ground-fault detection. Attic temperature and humidity sensors already exist, and any homeowner can buy one for $25. Snow depth sensors already exist, and the weather feed already forecasts rain on snow. New material here is the gradient, the dew point against sheathing temperature, the melt model from heat loss, the per-house learned baseline, and the signature table. A patent examiner could reasonably say that adding a thermometer to a heat cable controller is an ordinary engineering step, and that the Lstiburek and University of Minnesota guidance plus a few sensors gets most of the way there. That is a fair objection, and the claims are drawn to the fusion and the prediction precisely because the individual pieces are not new.

Second objection is practical. Air sealing and insulation are the fix, and they are also the diagnosis: a blower door test and an infrared camera find the bypass leaks in an afternoon, and a roofer with a rake finds the dam. A homeowner who air seals the attic, insulates to R-49 or R-60, clears the soffit vents, and rakes the lower roof after each storm will not dam, and will not need a sensor to tell them so. That price is worth paying only for houses that keep damming after the obvious fixes, for owners who cannot get into the attic, and for dams that form on slopes nobody watches. That is a real population, it includes most older houses in snow country, but it is not every house, and the disclosure should not pretend otherwise.

Third objection is that roof temperatures are messier than the table suggests. A deck temperature depends on the shingle color, the sun angle, the wind, the snow depth, and the insulation below it, and the gradient moves with the weather in ways that vary between houses and even between slopes on the same house. A signature table with clean rows is a design document, not a measurement, and the first fleet deployment would find rows that overlap and signatures that do not separate as cleanly as written. Two-standard-deviation rule and first-season learning period are the honest answer to that objection, and they are also an admission that the thresholds are a starting point, not a result.

## What a Homeowner Can Do Now

Attic safety first: walk only on joists or on boards laid across them, never on the drywall between joists, and do not go into the attic alone.

None of this requires buying anything, and the checks below take about 30 minutes on a cold day, plus a look in the attic. Here is what you can do now, before the snow season. They are the manual version of what the system does automatically, and doing them once before the snow season covers most of the risk.

1. **Take the attic temperature.** On a cold night, put a thermometer in the attic and one outside. A tight attic sits a few degrees above outdoor temperature. An attic running 10 to 20 degrees above outdoor, with the house at 70, is leaking house air, and the leak is the dam. Find it before the snow comes.
2. **Look for frost in the attic.** On a cold morning, look at the underside of the roof sheathing and the rafters. Frost on the wood means moist house air is condensing and freezing up there, and that frost melts in the spring and grows mold. Frost is the attic telling you the ceiling leaks air.
3. **Check the soffit vents from outside.** Walk around the house and look at the soffit vents. A vent buried by insulation, painted shut, or blocked by a bird nest is a vent that does not exist. Clear them, and fit baffles so the next insulation top-up does not bury them again.
4. **Rake the lower roof after each storm.** A roof rake pulls snow off the bottom 3 to 4 feet of the slope from the ground, which removes the snow that would have melted and refrozen at the eave. Work from the ground, pull down the slope, and stop if the rake catches a shingle edge.
5. **Look at the recessed lights from the attic.** Recessed can lights are the most common bypass leak in the ceiling. From the attic, look for light fixtures that are not airtight and not covered by insulation, and for gaps around plumbing stacks, chimneys, and the attic hatch. Seal them with caulk or foam, and keep insulation off non-IC-rated fixtures.
6. **Test the heat cable before the snow.** If the house has heat cable, plug it in on a cold day and feel whether it warms, and check whether its ground-fault protection holds. A cable that trips its protection in November will not work in January, and January is when the dam forms.
7. **Clear the gutters and downspouts.** A gutter full of leaves holds water, and a downspout frozen shut holds it longer. Clean both before the first snow, and confirm water runs out the bottom of each downspout with a hose.
8. **Check the ice barrier at the next re-roof.** When the roof is replaced, confirm the roofer installs a self-adhering ice and water shield from the lowest edge to at least 24 inches inside the exterior wall line. It is the second line of defense, and it only exists if someone puts it there.

Know a homeowner who has not looked in their attic since summer? Send them this page. Those checks take about thirty minutes.
