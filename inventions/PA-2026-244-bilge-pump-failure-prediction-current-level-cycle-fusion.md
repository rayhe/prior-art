## Abstract

Disclosed here is a system that predicts failure in recreational vessel bilge pumping systems and estimates dockside flooding risk before water reaches the cabin sole. Four channels fuse on an edge processor: bilge water level measured continuously, pump motor current sampled through each run, battery voltage under load and at rest, and pump cycle history, with per-cycle duration and interval, derived from the level and current records rather than sensed directly. Each channel is read per pump cycle and trended across the season against a per-boat learned baseline. A weather-data feed supplies rainfall and forecast temperature, and an oil-detection input reads an existing oil-detecting switch and inhibits pumping when oil is present.

A signature table maps combinations of channel deviations to eleven specific failure modes, distinguishing faults that present identically to an owner, such as a stuck-closed float switch versus a blown fuse, and a slow hull leak versus rain ingress through a blocked cockpit drain. It estimates inflow rate from cycle timing, projects time to flooding from battery state of charge, computes a composite Bilge Health Index, issues part-level diagnoses with confidence scores, and shares anonymized failure signatures across a fleet to detect bad production batches and regional weather-driven patterns. As a retrofit, sensors clamp onto existing pump wiring and mount in the existing bilge, with no change to the hull, the pump, or the discharge plumbing.

## Field of the Invention

This disclosure covers diagnostics for recreational boat bilge systems: multi-sensor fusion and edge inference that tell apart pump, switch, electrical, and hull-integrity failures in a boat sitting unattended at dock or mooring.

## Background

### How a Bilge System Works

A boat owner knows this machine, and so does any homeowner with a basement: a bilge pump is a sump pump for a house nobody visits. A recreational boat is a hull with a dozen small holes in it on purpose. Through-hull fittings admit cooling water, drain sinks, and discharge waste. A sterndrive passes through a rubber bellows. A propeller shaft passes through a seal. Every one of those penetrations is a controlled leak, and the bilge pump is the last defense when control slips. Water that gets in collects in the bilge, the lowest part of the hull. A float switch, or a level sensor in an automatic pump, turns the pump on when the water reaches a set height. Once running, the pump pushes the water up a discharge hose and out a through-hull fitting above the waterline. A check valve in some installations stops the water in the hose from running back into the bilge when the pump stops. A second, larger pump and a high-water alarm sit higher in the bilge as a backup. Power comes from the house battery bank, which a shore-power charger tops up while the boat sits in its slip.

Scale matters. The United States Coast Guard counted 11,674,073 registered recreational vessels in 2024, up 1.1 percent on the year, with Florida alone holding 1,171,432. The National Marine Manufacturers Association estimates another 3.6 million boats that are neither registered nor documented, mostly small craft, which puts the total fleet at about 15.3 million vessels. That figure counts state registrations plus the unregistered fleet and leaves out roughly 130,000 federally documented vessels, which is why the NMMA's own total, 11.8 million registered and documented plus 3.6 million, comes to about 15.4 million. Most of those boats sit unattended for most of the week. A boat in a slip is visited on weekends, if that, and the bilge pump is the only crew member on board between visits. Almost all of that fleet is diagnosed the same way: an owner lifts a hatch, looks at the bilge, and guesses.

### The Sinking Numbers

BoatUS, which runs one of the largest recreational boat insurance programs in North America, analyzed a year of its sinking claims in 2014, and the findings have held up across its later reviews. Of the boats in those claims, 69 percent sank at the dock or on a mooring, not underway. Of the dockside sinkings, 39 percent happened when a small part failed through wear, tear, or corrosion, and the gradual failure of a part below the waterline was the single most common reason boats sank at the dock. Boating Magazine, reading the same BoatUS claims data, put the share of dockside sinkings out of the owner's hands at 35 percent. The two figures are different cuts of the same claims, not two slices of one pie: 39 percent names a cause, gradual failure of a small part, while 35 percent names a judgment, how much was beyond the owner's control. A part can fail gradually and still have been preventable, which is why the two numbers do not add to 100 and the gap between them is not a missing category.

BoatUS reports these as proportions of the claims it analyzed, and does not publish an annual count of sinkings in that analysis, so the percentages describe the shape of the problem, not its size in boats per year. Most dockside sinkings were preventable, and the prevention was usually maintenance that nobody did because nothing told them to do it.

BoatUS draws a sharp line that most owners miss. A well-designed boat should stay afloat in the conditions it was designed for without water having to be pumped out of it, even in heavy rain. The bilge pump is there to remove nuisance water and to buy time while a leak is found and fixed. It is not a substitute for a watertight hull. Yet the claims files are full of boats that existed for weeks in what BoatUS calls a zombie state, somewhere between floating and sinking, completely dependent on the bilge pump to stay on the water instead of below it. In those cases the pump merely postponed the sinking until it failed, lost power, or was overwhelmed by the volume of water. Beth Leonard, writing up the analysis in BoatUS Seaworthy, put the human side of it plainly: nine out of ten people who call to report a sinking say the bilge pump stopped working. That is not why the boat sank. The boat sank because a small leak went unnoticed for weeks, and the pump was the only thing standing between that leak and the bottom.

Underway sinkings follow a different pattern. About 43 percent of underway sinkings happen because the boat hit something, a log, the bottom, or another boat. In the BoatUS claims, sterndrives account for more than a quarter of all part-failure-related sinkings, and eight out of every ten sterndrive failures involve damaged bellows. A bellows is a rubber boot that seals the sterndrive where it passes through the transom. It flexes every time the drive steers or trims, it sits in salt water, and it is usually inspected only when the boat is hauled. When it splits, water enters at a rate no recreational bilge pump can match, and the owner finds out when the boat sits lower in the water or not at all.

Losses are not rare in absolute terms either, across all reportable boating incidents, not sinkings alone. The Coast Guard counted 3,887 reportable recreational boating incidents in 2024, with 556 deaths and roughly $88 million in property damage, and machinery failure ranked among the top five primary contributing factors. Sinkings are the costliest category of hull claim in the BoatUS files, and a sunk boat in a slip is often a total loss even when it is raised, because salt water in the engine, the wiring, and the interior adds up faster than the hull is worth.

**The numbers:** 69% of boats that sink do it at the dock or on a mooring (BoatUS claims analysis, 2014). 39% of dockside sinkings trace to gradual failure of a small part below the waterline (BoatUS claims analysis, 2014). ### The Battery Side: The Pump Is Only as Good as Its Power

A bilge pump is a 12-volt direct-current motor, and at the dock it runs on a battery that nobody is watching. A typical Group 27 deep-cycle marine battery is rated near 100 amp-hours, and only about half of that is usable before the voltage sags low enough to matter, so call it 50 usable amp-hours. A Rule 2000 pump, the most common large recreational bilge pump, draws 8.4 amps at 12 volts and 12 amps at 13.6 volts, which is the voltage a charger holds the bank at. At 8.4 amps, 50 usable amp-hours lasts about six hours of continuous pumping. That sounds like a lot until the duty cycle is counted. A boat with a slow leak that makes the pump run two minutes an hour draws about 0.28 amp-hours per hour, and the battery lasts about a week. At five minutes an hour, the draw is 0.7 amp-hours per hour and the battery lasts about three days. At fifteen minutes an hour, the battery is dead in about a day. Shore power covers all of this while the charger works. When the charger fails, when the shore-power cord is unplugged, or when the breaker trips, the countdown starts silently, and the owner finds out when the boat is already low in the water.

A float switch stuck open is worse. A stuck-open switch leaves the pump running dry, drawing roughly 4 to 6 amps with no water to move, and 50 usable amp-hours is gone in about eight to twelve hours. A dry-running pump also overheats. Its impeller is cooled and lubricated by the water it moves, and running dry scores the impeller housing and can seize the motor. So a single stuck switch can kill the pump and the battery in the same night, which is exactly the failure that shows up in the claims files as a boat found sitting on the bottom of its slip on a Monday morning.

Cold makes it worse. Battery capacity falls as temperature drops, and a battery at 32 degrees Fahrenheit holds roughly 80 percent of the capacity it holds at 77 degrees. Winter is when boats sit longest between visits, when rain and snow add the most water, and when batteries are weakest. That combination is why dockside sinkings cluster in the wet months, and why a system that watches voltage trend, not just instantaneous voltage, is the difference between a warning on Tuesday and a salvage call on Saturday.

### The Pump-Rating Gap: The Number on the Box Is Not the Number in the Bilge

Bilge pumps are sold by a big number followed by GPH, gallons per hour. That number is the open-flow rating, measured in a lab with zero resistance, and it is a marketing number, not a performance number. The moment the pump has to push water up a hose and out of the hull, the flow falls. The American Boat and Yacht Council standard H-22 requires compliant makers to rate pumps at heads of zero, one meter, and two meters, and the published tables tell the story. A Rule 2000 rated at 2,000 gallons per hour delivers 1,620 at one meter of head and 1,300 at two meters, at 13.6 volts after a ten-hour break-in. An ABYC educational infographic puts the same class of pump at about 1,275 gallons per hour in real-world conditions, roughly two-thirds of the number on the box. A Rule 1500 falls from 1,500 to 1,200 at one meter of head and to 680 at two meters. Small submersible pumps are rarely useful with more than four feet of vertical discharge head, and medium and large submersibles are similarly ineffective past seven feet, according to Practical Sailor's testing notes.

That gap matters because inflow is unforgiving. A 3-inch hole 2.5 feet below the waterline admits about 10,100 gallons per hour, nearly eight times what a 2,000-gallon-per-hour pump actually moves, according to an ABYC illustration. A smaller hole is still enough to matter. A 1-inch hole 2 feet below the waterline admits about 1,670 gallons per hour by Torricelli's law, and about 1,000 gallons per hour once a realistic discharge coefficient of 0.6 is applied for a sharp-edged opening. Against the adjusted figure of about 1,000 gallons per hour, a Rule 1500 moving 1,200 gallons per hour at one meter of head has a margin of about 20 percent, and against the ideal 1,670-gallon figure it has none at all. Either way there is no margin left for a tired battery, a partially clogged strainer, or a hose with a kink in it.

The smaller pump also loses a larger share of its flow to head than the Rule 2000 does: at two meters the 1500 keeps only 680 of its 1,500 gallons per hour, 45 percent, while the 2000 keeps 1,300 of 2,000, 65 percent, because the same lift consumes a bigger fraction of the pressure a smaller pump can generate. A recreational bilge pump handles nuisance water and small seeps. It does not handle hull damage. A system that measures inflow rate, rather than waiting for the water to reach an alarm float, can tell the owner that the leak has crossed from nuisance to structural while there is still time to haul the boat.

Ratings are also measured at full voltage. A pump fed 11 volts instead of 13.6 moves materially less water, and a pump fed through corroded wiring sees less voltage still. Voltage drop under load is the signature of that problem, and it is invisible to every device that only checks whether the pump is running. Current, voltage, and water level read together are what separate a pump that is working from a pump that is merely spinning.

### How It Fails: Why Eleven Failures Feel Like One

Every bilge failure an owner notices looks the same from the dock: water in the bilge, or a boat sitting low, or a pump that runs all the time. Eleven different faults produce that one symptom, and they need eleven different fixes. A stuck float switch needs a new switch. A clogged impeller needs cleaning. A seized motor needs a new pump. A split bellows needs a haul-out. A dead battery needs a charger. Treating them as one problem, usually by fitting a bigger pump, fixes none of them, and a bigger pump on a dying battery just drains the battery faster.

Here are the eleven, in the order a diagnostic system would meet them. The full channel pattern for each is in the signature table in §7.

1. **Float switch stuck closed.** The pump never starts; the level rises while current stays zero.
2. **Float switch stuck open.** The pump runs dry around the clock; current stays low and steady, and battery voltage falls in a straight line.
3. **Clogged strainer or impeller.** The pump runs, current reads near normal, and the level does not fall.
4. **Seized motor or jammed impeller.** Locked-rotor current spikes, then zero as the fuse blows or the thermal cutout trips.
5. **Bearing and motor wear.** Mean current creeps up week over week, ripple grows, and cycles lengthen for the same water.
6. **Blocked or kinked discharge hose, or a stuck check valve.** Current rises above baseline while the level stays flat.
7. **Below-waterline fitting failure.** Cycle frequency climbs week over week while each cycle stays short.
8. **Battery depletion.** Voltage under load sags further each cycle, and each cycle moves less water.
9. **Shore-power charger loss.** Resting voltage trends down day over day instead of holding near 13.6.
10. **Rain and cockpit drain blockage.** Inflow spikes after rain and correlates with rainfall, not with engine hours.
11. **High-water sensor fouled or alarm failed.** The level and the cycle history disagree with the alarm, and no alert fires as the water rises.

Oil in the bilge deserves a note of its own. A boat with an engine leak can have a layer of oil or fuel floating on the bilge water, and pumping that mixture overboard is prohibited: discharge of oil into navigable waters is unlawful under the Clean Water Act, section 311 ([33 U.S.C. § 1321](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/33/1321)), which prohibits discharges in harmful quantities, including any that produces a visible sheen, and a spill carries reporting and cleanup duties. Existing oil-detecting switches already refuse to pump when oil is present. What they do not do is diagnose why the oil is there, or whether the pump, the switch, and the battery are healthy underneath the oil layer. This system reads the output of an existing oil-detecting switch as an interlock input: when oil is present, it inhibits pumping, flags the oil, and instructs containment with an absorbent pad and notification of the marina, while still diagnosing the pump, the switch, and the battery underneath the oil layer. Holding the pump off while oil is present means the bilge may take on water while containment is arranged, so the marina's response is urgent, not routine.

### What Exists Already

Boat owners are not without tools, and the gap this disclosure fills is specific, not total. Naming what exists is part of the disclosure, because the new contribution sits in the space between these products.

Mechanical float switches are the baseline. A Rule-A-Matic switch is a buoyant lever that closes a contact when the water lifts it, rated to 14 amps at 12 volts, and the heavier Super Switch is rated to 20 amps. They are cheap, simple, and the single most failure-prone part of the system, because they sit in the dirtiest water on the boat and their pivots corrode, their floats fill with water through pinholes, and their contacts oxidize. A 1998 patent, US 5,793,294, is an electrode-based sump alarm that uses dissimilar metals instead of a float, and its background discusses the failure modes of the prior float switches it replaces: floats that develop pinholes and fill, buoyant material that saturates and loses buoyancy, pivots that stick, and contacts that oxidize.

Automatic pumps remove the separate switch. Rule's automatic pumps check for water every two and a half minutes by running briefly and measuring load against the impeller, and they draw about 0.25 amp-hours per day in that cycling mode. They solve the stuck-float problem by removing the float, and they introduce a new one, which is that a pump that cycles every two and a half minutes all day is also a pump whose owner cannot tell from its sound whether it is pumping water or air.

Blue Guard Innovations sells the closest thing to a smart bilge switch. The BG-One, about $219 as of November 2026, is a solid-state switch with oil and fuel detection that refuses to pump oil overboard, with modes for marinas, marine sanctuaries, and open water. Its Bluetooth app reports bilge temperature, voltage at the switch, and current consumed by the pump. Its control panel, the BG-CP, shows a pump cycle count that increments on each power cycle up to 99 and then rolls over, plus latched oil and high-water alarms. The BG-One is a better switch, and it reports live readings. It does not learn a baseline, it does not estimate inflow rate, it does not project time to flooding, and its cycle count resets at 99 and carries no duration or interval history.

Remote boat monitors cover the alerting side. Siren Marine's wireless bilge sensor connects to the bilge pump and alerts the owner through an app when the pump turns on, turns off, or runs for too long, and it shows pump history and battery state. GOST and Boat Command sell similar connected-boat systems. These products answer the question "is something wrong right now" for an owner who is somewhere else. They do not answer the question "which part is failing, and how long do I have," because they do not fuse current, level, cycle timing, and voltage into a per-boat baseline, and they do not estimate inflow or project flooding time from battery state.

Patents cover pieces of this too. US 6,469,641, granted to Lash in 2002, describes a marine vessel monitoring system that detects a blown bilge fuse or a low battery before high water occurs, and transmits the warning over a communication network. US 7,327,286, granted to Yacht Watchman, describes remote monitoring of bilge pump operation, smoke and fire detectors, and battery voltage through a telemetry unit. The closest filing is a Brunswick application, US 2022/0154720, published May 19, 2022, which describes a bilge pump controller that determines from pump current whether the pump system is malfunctioning, diagnoses conditions including a clogged outlet, a major leak, heavy rain, a blown fuse, a failed harness, an airlock, a stalled motor, and a stuck impeller, runs a predetermined routine to rectify the malfunction, and falls back to a second pump.

Brunswick's application is a real and recent piece of prior art, and it covers current-based malfunction diagnosis with rectification routines. It does not disclose mm-resolution continuous level trending fused with a per-boat learned baseline, inflow-rate estimation from cycle timing, leak-versus-rain discrimination using weather data, time-to-flooding projection from battery state of charge, or fleet learning across boats. Brunswick's drawings already show a water-level sensor and plot current against cycle time and duration, so the boundary is not whether level is sensed at all, but whether the level is trended in millimeters against a learned baseline and turned into an inflow estimate.

### The Search

Before writing this disclosure, the existing corpus was checked for overlap. The 248 prior disclosures on this site include sump pump diagnostics, pool equipment diagnostics, water softener fault diagnosis, and well pump short-cycling diagnosis, and none of them covers a marine bilge system. The distinguishing facts are the operating environment and the failure economics: a bilge pump runs unattended on a finite battery, in salt water, in a hull that is itself the thing being protected, and the cost of a missed diagnosis is the boat. ## Detailed Description

### 1. System Overview

As a retrofit module, it mounts in the bilge area and wires into the existing pump circuit without cutting the discharge hose or replacing the pump. Four channels feed an edge processor, a low-power microcontroller with a cellular or marina Wi-Fi radio, mounted above the maximum bilge water level in a sealed enclosure. A continuous water-level sensor, an ultrasonic sensor mounted above the bilge or a pressure transducer mounted at the bilge floor, measures water depth in millimeters rather than reporting a binary wet-or-dry state. A split-core Hall-effect current sensor clamps around the pump positive lead and measures current without breaking the circuit. A voltage tap at the battery, fused within 7 inches of the battery post per ABYC E-11, measures battery voltage under load and at rest. Pump cycle history, per-cycle duration and interval, is derived from the level and current records rather than sensed directly, which makes it the fourth channel.

Two further inputs are not sensor channels: a weather-data feed, either a rain gauge on the boat or a marina weather service over the radio link, supplies rainfall and forecast air temperature, and an oil-detection input reads the output of an existing oil-detecting switch. When the oil input reports oil present, the processor inhibits pump operation, flags the oil, and instructs containment and marina notification instead of pumping.

The processor samples the current sensor at 1 kilohertz during pump runs, records the level every 10 seconds, logs each pump cycle with start time, duration, and peak current, and stores a rolling 90-day history in flash memory. All diagnosis runs on the edge processor, so the system works when the radio link is down, and the radio is used for alerts, for fetching weather, and for sharing anonymized signatures with the fleet service.

Parts cost for the four channels is about $150 to $250 at retail quantities: a Hall-effect current sensor near $15, an ultrasonic level sensor near $25, a voltage divider and fuse near $10, a microcontroller board with radio near $20, and a sealed enclosure, cabling, and mounting hardware near $50. Installation is about an hour for a competent owner, and about two hours billed at a yard, with no haul-out required. Nothing here replaces the float switch, the pump, or the high-water alarm. It watches them.

### 2. Water Level: The Inflow Channel

Continuous level measurement is the channel that turns a bilge pump from a reactive device into a diagnostic instrument. A binary float switch reports one bit, wet or dry. A level sensor reporting depth every 10 seconds gives a curve, and the slope of that curve is the inflow rate. With the pump off and the level rising 2 millimeters per hour, the boat takes on about 0.4 gallons per hour for a typical bilge footprint of 8 square feet, since one millimeter of depth over 8 square feet is about 0.2 gallons. At 10 millimeters per hour, inflow is about 2 gallons per hour, which is a drip that has become a leak. If the level rises faster than the pump can lower it during a run, the inflow exceeds pump capacity and the boat is in trouble no matter how healthy the pump is. Level also measures what the pump actually accomplishes: a run that starts at 60 millimeters and ends at 35 millimeters moved 25 millimeters of water, about 5 gallons, in roughly 15 seconds at 20 gallons per minute, while a run that starts at 60 and ends at 55 moved about 1 gallon, which points at a clogged impeller or a blocked hose rather than at the switch.

Level measurement needs two guards against false readings. First, the sensor must reject slosh. A boat in a slip moves with wakes and wind, and the water surface tilts and oscillates. A 60-second median filter applied to the raw level readings removes wave action while preserving the slow rise of a real leak. Second, the sensor must survive fouling. An ultrasonic sensor mounted above the water never touches the bilge, which avoids the oil and growth that foul submerged probes, and a pressure transducer at the floor is specified with a flush diaphragm that sheds debris. Either sensor is calibrated once at installation against two known depths, the pump-on level and the pump-off level, and the calibration is rechecked automatically whenever the owner runs the manual pump test described in the owner checks below.

### 3. Pump Current: The Mechanical Channel

Motor current is the richest single signal in the system, because a direct-current pump motor reports its mechanical load through its current draw. From every run it records three features: startup inrush, mean running current, and high-frequency ripple.

Startup inrush is the brief current spike when the motor starts from rest, typically two to four times the running current for 100 to 300 milliseconds. Take a healthy Rule 2000 drawing 8.4 amps running: its inrush lands near 20 to 30 amps. When the motor seizes, the locked-rotor inrush stays high for a second or more before the fuse blows or the thermal cutout trips, and the processor flags any inrush lasting longer than one second as a jammed-impeller event. No inrush at all means a blown fuse, which separates a dead pump from a stuck float switch, since a stuck switch also shows no current but the level keeps rising.

Mean running current is compared against the per-boat learned baseline for the same pump. For a Rule 2000 that baseline sits near 8.4 amps at 12 volts. When the mean creeps up slowly, about 0.2 amps a month with no change in level behavior, the bearings are wearing or the impeller is fouling, and the processor issues a service warning once mean current rises 15 percent above baseline. A drop to roughly half the baseline with the level falling slowly means the pump is running dry or cavitating, since an impeller spinning in air or in a foam of air and water draws far less current than one moving solid water. And a rise of 25 percent or more with the level flat means the pump is pushing against a blocked hose or a stuck check valve.

High-frequency ripple is the alternating-current component riding on the direct-current draw, produced by the motor commutator switching as the armature turns. In a healthy motor the ripple sits near the commutation frequency, the motor speed times the number of commutator segments divided by 60, and stays stable. As bearings roughen, the ripple amplitude grows and its spectrum broadens; as a brush wears, the ripple turns irregular. To see that spectrum the current is sampled at 1 kilohertz during pump runs, which keeps the commutation frequency, roughly 100 to 400 hertz for a small pump motor, below the 500-hertz Nyquist limit. Ripple amplitude is computed as the root-mean-square of the current signal after removing the mean, and trended across the season. Ripple is the earliest warning channel in the system, because it changes before mean current moves and before the pump moves measurably less water.

Current also has to be corrected for voltage. A pump fed 11 volts draws less current and moves less water than the same pump fed 13.6 volts, and a current reading without a simultaneous voltage reading is ambiguous. Voltage is recorded with every current sample, and the current is normalized to 12 volts using the motor's approximate voltage-current relationship, so a current fall caused by a sagging battery is attributed to the battery, not to the pump.

### 4. Cycle History: The Inflow-Rate Channel

Cycle history is the channel that catches the leak, as opposed to the pump. Every pump cycle has a start time, a duration, and an interval since the previous cycle, and the pattern of those three numbers over days is a fingerprint of the boat's water ingress. A sound boat at dock in dry weather cycles once or twice a week. Each cycle lasts roughly fifteen seconds and moves about 5 gallons, the water that collected since the last cycle from a shaft seal drip, condensation, and rain through a hatch, for a total of about 5 to 10 gallons a week. A boat with a slow leak cycles more often, with the same short duration, because the pump still works and the bilge just refills faster. A boat with a failing pump cycles less often or for longer, because each cycle moves less water.

Inflow rate is estimated from cycle timing without a flow meter, using total pump run time per period. If the pump moves about 20 gallons per minute at its installed head, and it runs 2 minutes total every hour, the boat takes on about 40 gallons per hour, which is a leak that needs a haul-out within days. If it runs 2 minutes total every 6 hours, inflow is about 7 gallons per hour, which is a leak that needs a visit within the week. If it runs 2 minutes total once a day, inflow is about 1.7 gallons per hour, about 40 gallons a day, which is a small leak worth a visit within a couple of weeks, not normal seepage. Normal seepage is far smaller: a sound boat's pump runs about fifteen seconds once or twice a week, which is 5 to 10 gallons a week, or about 0.05 gallons per hour. The estimate uses the pump's measured effective flow rate, which the system learns by dividing the level drop during a run by the run duration, rather than the number on the box. A pump that has lost 20 percent of its flow to wear still produces an honest inflow estimate, because the estimate uses the pump the boat actually has.

Cycle history also separates a leak from rain. Rain inflow correlates with rainfall in the weather feed, with a lag of minutes, and it stops when the rain stops. A hull leak does not correlate with rain and does not stop. Cycle frequency and rainfall are correlated over a rolling 14-day window, and a high correlation with a dry-weather baseline near zero points at cockpit drains and hatch seals, while a low correlation with a rising dry-weather baseline points at the hull. That distinction decides whether the owner climbs on deck with a hose or calls the yard for a haul-out, and getting it wrong wastes a weekend or a boat.

### 5. Battery Voltage: The Energy Channel

Voltage is read in two states: at rest, with the pump off and no other loads, and under load, during a pump run. At rest, near 12.6 volts means a full 12-volt lead-acid battery, 12.2 volts is about half charge, and 11.8 volts is effectively empty for pumping purposes, since the pump slows and moves little water below that point. Under load, voltage sags below its resting value by an amount that depends on the battery's internal resistance and the current draw: a healthy Group 27 sags about 0.3 to 0.5 volts under an 8.4-amp pump load, while a sulfated or aged battery sags 1 volt or more, and the sag grows month over month as the battery ages. Both the resting voltage and the sag are trended. A battery warning goes out when the sag exceeds 0.8 volts, or when resting voltage falls below 12.2 volts with shore power connected, which indicates the charger is not doing its job. The 0.8-volt warning threshold is deliberately lower than the 1-volt rule of thumb in the owner checks below: the system warns early and watches the trend, while a single manual reading needs a larger margin before it means anything.

Shore-power loss is detected from the resting voltage trend. With a working charger, resting voltage holds near 13.6 volts, the float voltage. When the charger stops, resting voltage decays toward the battery's open-circuit voltage and then falls further as the pump cycles. A decay of more than 0.5 volts over 48 hours with no corresponding increase in pump cycling indicates the charger or the shore-power connection has failed, and the system alerts before the battery is low enough to matter. The alert includes an estimate of remaining pump-hours, computed from the usable capacity implied by the voltage and the recent average pump duty cycle, so the owner knows whether to drive to the marina tonight or on Saturday.

### 6. Weather: The Rain Channel

Weather data arrives over the radio link from a marina weather service, or from a small rain gauge and temperature sensor mounted on the boat. It serves two purposes: the leak-versus-rain discrimination described above, and a freeze warning. Water in a discharge hose can freeze in the hose and block the pump, and water in the bilge can freeze around the pump and the float switch. When the air temperature forecast falls below 36 degrees Fahrenheit and the boat is in the water, the system runs a freeze check: it cycles the pump briefly and watches the level fall, confirming the hose is clear, and it alerts if the level does not fall, which indicates ice. The freeze check does not run when the oil-detection input reports oil present, since pumping is inhibited then. It also alerts if the bilge water temperature, measured by a sensor on the level transducer, falls below 35 degrees, since a frozen bilge is a bilge the pump cannot protect.

Rainfall also sets the expected cycle count for a dry boat. One inch of rain on a 60-square-foot cockpit puts about 37 gallons of water in the boat, and on a 100-square-foot cockpit and deck catchment it puts about 62 gallons. A Rule 2000 moving 1,300 gallons per hour at two meters of head clears 62 gallons in about three minutes of pumping. Forecast rainfall and the boat's catchment area, entered at installation, predict the cycle count for a storm, and a cycle count far above the prediction indicates a drain is blocked or a hatch is leaking, while a cycle count far below it indicates the pump is not keeping up.

### 7. Edge Diagnostic: The Signature Table

The diagnostic is a signature table, not a black-box model. Rows are failure modes, columns are channels, and each cell states which way that channel should deviate from the boat's own baseline. Scoring counts how many cells match what the boat is actually doing, weighted by how distinctive each cell is, and the best-matching rows come back with a confidence score. Three matching channels with high distinctiveness is a diagnosis; one matching channel is a watch item. Nothing is diagnosed on a single channel alone, because every single-channel reading has a benign explanation.

| Failure mode | Water level | Pump current | Cycle pattern | Battery voltage |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Float switch stuck closed ⚠ | **Rising, pump idle** | Zero | No cycles | No change |
| Float switch stuck open | Low, flat | Low, steady, dry-run | **Continuous run** | Falling fast |
| Clogged strainer or impeller | **Falls slowly or flat** | Normal to low | Long runs | No change |
| Seized motor or jammed impeller | Rising | **Locked-rotor spike, then zero** | One short attempt | Brief sag, then normal |
| Bearing and motor wear | Falls slowly | **Mean rising, ripple rising** | Runs lengthening | No change |
| Blocked hose or stuck check valve | Flat | **Rising 25 percent or more** | Long runs, no level change | No change |
| Below-waterline fitting failure ⚠ | Rises between cycles | Normal per run | **Frequency climbing weekly** | Normal, then sagging |
| Battery depletion | Falls slowly per run | Falling with voltage | Runs lengthening | **Sag growing, rest falling** |
| Shore-power charger loss | Normal | Normal | Normal | **Rest decaying over days** |
| Rain or blocked cockpit drain | Spikes after rain | Normal per run | **Correlates with rainfall** | No change |
| High-water sensor fouled | Above alarm level | Normal or zero | **No alarm despite level** | No change |

The two marked rows deserve emphasis because they are the ones that sink boats. A below-waterline fitting failure shows up first as cycle frequency, not as water, because the pump still works and the level never gets high. That pattern gets flagged when the dry-weather cycle count rises 50 percent above the learned baseline for two consecutive weeks, which is weeks before the water reaches the cabin sole. A stuck-closed float switch shows up as the opposite pattern, a rising level with zero current, and the system treats that combination as an urgent alert rather than a watch item, because the primary pump is not running and the only defense left is the high-water alarm.

Time-to-flooding projection is the output that turns a diagnosis into a decision. Inflow rate comes from the cycle timing, remaining pumping capacity from the battery state of charge and the pump's measured effective flow rate, and the time until the water reaches a critical level, defined as the cabin sole or the top of the battery boxes, whichever is lower. If inflow exceeds pump capacity, the projection is measured in hours and the alert says so. If the battery will die before the leak is fixed, the projection is the earlier of the two times, flooding or battery exhaustion. The projection is recomputed after every cycle, and it is labeled as an estimate with the inflow rate and battery state it was computed from, so the owner can see the reasoning instead of trusting a number.

### 8. Bilge Health Index

Four channels combine into a single Bilge Health Index from 0 to 100, presented with the channel and signature responsible for each point lost. The index starts at 100. The hydraulic subscore loses points for inflow rate above baseline, for level rise during a pump run, and for cycle frequency above baseline. The mechanical subscore loses points for mean current deviation, ripple growth, and inrush anomalies. The energy subscore loses points for voltage sag, resting voltage decay, and projected pump-hours below 48. The sensor subscore loses points when the level channel and the cycle history disagree, which indicates a fouled sensor rather than a failing pump.

Each subscore is shown separately. An index below 80 triggers a service warning, below 60 triggers an urgent alert, and below 40 triggers a critical alert with a time-to-flooding estimate. The 80/60/40 thresholds are reasoned starting points, not measured results: they have not been validated against a fleet of real boats, and every index is labeled as an estimate. The index does not replace manual checks: lifting the float switch, inspecting the bellows, and hauling the boat on schedule. The thresholds are defaults, and the owner can tighten them.

**Bilge Health Index, worked example: 62.** The index starts at 100. Hydraulic subscore, minus 15: inflow 40 percent above baseline and cycle frequency climbing. Mechanical subscore, minus 10: mean current 18 percent above baseline with ripple growing. Energy subscore, minus 8: voltage sag of 0.9 volts and resting voltage at 12.3 volts. Sensor subscore, minus 5: level and cycle history disagreed on two days. 100 minus 38 is 62, a service warning, and the largest loss is hydraulic, so the problem is the hull, not the pump or the battery.

### 9. Baseline Learning: The First Two Weeks

Every boat is different, and a fixed threshold that fits a 20-foot center console will false-alarm on a 40-foot trawler and miss a leak on a sailboat. So the baseline is learned from the boat it is installed on. For the first 14 days after installation, it records without diagnosing: dry-weather cycles per day, mean cycle duration, mean running current and inrush, ripple amplitude, resting voltage, and voltage sag under load. Fourteen days is long enough to include several dry days, and on a boat that cycles a few times a day it is long enough to collect at least 30 cycles, and short enough that the owner gets diagnoses in the same season the system is installed. A sound boat cycles only once or twice a week, so on a quiet boat the learning period stretches for weeks, fifteen to thirty of them at that cadence, before 30 cycles accumulate and diagnoses begin. It requires at least 30 pump cycles and at least 3 dry days in that window, and if the boat does not cycle enough in 14 days, the learning period extends until it does.

The baseline is stored as a mean and a standard deviation for each metric, and a deviation is counted only when it exceeds two standard deviations from the mean, which keeps ordinary variation from generating alerts. That two-standard-deviation rule is a starting point, not a measured result: with only a few dozen cycles in the learning window, the sample spread is itself uncertain, and cycle data are skewed rather than normal, so the rule will need tuning against fleet data before its false-alarm rate is known.

Baselines are relearned after a known change. When the owner replaces the pump, the switch, or the battery, they press a button in the app or on the module, and the system starts a new 14-day learning period for the affected channels. When the boat is hauled and relaunched, the system pauses diagnosis for 48 hours, because a boat on the hard has no bilge water and a boat just launched has a wet bilge from the launch itself. Seasonal drift is handled by a slow update: the baseline mean moves by 1 percent of the difference between the old mean and the new week's mean each week, which tracks gradual wear without absorbing a sudden failure into the baseline. The drift is bounded on purpose: the baseline moves 1 percent of the gap per week, while the fitting-failure flag in §7 trips at a 50 percent rise sustained over two weeks. A leak growing by less than about a third of a percent a week could be absorbed into the baseline, and at that rate it would take years to double; anything growing faster outruns the drift and trips the flag.

### 10. Display, Gateway, and Fleet Learning

The owner sees the system in three places. A small display at the helm or in the cabin shows the Bilge Health Index, the current level in millimeters, the cycles in the last 24 hours, and the battery voltage. A phone app shows the same, plus the signature table with the matching rows highlighted, the time-to-flooding projection, and the 90-day trend charts for each channel. An alert goes out by text message and push notification when the index crosses a threshold or when a signature matches with high confidence, and the alert names the suspected part, not just the symptom: "Float switch stuck closed, water rising, pump not running," rather than "Bilge alarm."

Fleet learning is the part that helps boats the system has never seen. Anonymized signatures, with no boat identity, no location finer than a marina, and no owner information, are shared with a fleet service. When a batch of float switches from one manufacturer starts sticking closed at three years of age across dozens of boats, the service can warn every owner with that switch before theirs sticks. When a marina's boats all show a cycle-frequency spike after the same storm, the service can separate a weather event from a hull problem for each boat individually. When a pump model shows a characteristic ripple pattern before bearing failure, the service can publish that pattern as a signature update, and every installed system learns to watch for it. Participation is opt-in, and a boat that never connects to the radio still gets the full local diagnostic, just without the fleet updates.

### 11. Scoring Rejection Rules

A diagnostic that cries wolf gets ignored, and a bilge diagnostic that cries wolf gets uninstalled. Diagnosis is therefore held back in conditions the system knows are not faults:

- **Priming:** the first 30 seconds of a pump run, while the pump primes and the current settles.
- **Washdown:** when the owner is hosing the deck and the bilge fills on purpose, detected from a rapid level rise combined with the owner pressing the washdown button, or from a level rise that starts at the deck wash time.
- **Underway:** detected from an accelerometer or from engine-run voltage, because a boat underway takes on spray and the pump cycles differently, and because a leak underway is a different problem with a different response.
- **After launch:** 48 hours, because a boat on the hard has no bilge water and a boat just launched has a wet bilge from the launch itself.
- **On a trailer:** detected from the absence of water around the hull sensor or from the owner's trailer mode.

A diagnosis held back in these conditions is logged, not discarded, so the owner can see what was held back and why. A pattern of repeated hold-backs is itself reported, because a boat that needs its diagnosis held back for washdown every day has a deck leak, not a washdown habit.

## Implementation Notes

Installation follows ABYC practice throughout. The current sensor clamps around the pump positive lead without cutting it, so the pump circuit keeps its existing fuse and wiring. The voltage tap is fused within 7 inches of the battery post, per ABYC E-11. All connections are made above the maximum bilge water level, with marine-grade butt connectors and sealant, because a connection that sits in bilge water corrodes and the corrosion shows up as voltage drop under load, which the system would then misread as a battery problem. The module enclosure is sealed to IP67, mounted above the high-water alarm level, and the level sensor cable is routed so it cannot chafe on a bulkhead. Fuse size stays as the pump maker specifies, since an oversized fuse turns a jammed impeller into a fire instead of a blown fuse.

Ignition protection matters. A gasoline boat's bilge can hold fuel vapor, and any electrical device in the bilge must meet ISO 8846 for ignition protection. The module is specified with sealed, ignition-protected construction, and the current sensor is a non-contact Hall-effect device, so there are no switching contacts in the bilge. Diesel boats have a lower vapor risk, and the same construction is used for both, because a part that is safe in one boat and dangerous in another is a part that will eventually be installed in the wrong boat.

Integration with existing boat networks is through NMEA 2000, which carries the Bilge Health Index, the level, and the alert state onto any multifunction display on the helm, and through a cellular or marina Wi-Fi radio for alerts to the owner's phone. A boat with no network still gets the full local diagnostic and a display at the helm. Power draw of the module itself is estimated at under 50 milliamps at 12 volts, about 0.6 watts, which is less than the self-discharge of the battery it watches, so the monitor does not become the load that sinks the boat.

## Limitations

This system diagnoses the bilge pumping system, not the hull. It cannot detect a leak that does not reach the bilge, such as a leak into a sealed compartment or a cored deck, and it cannot detect hull damage that admits water faster than any pump can remove it. A 3-inch hole below the waterline admits about 10,100 gallons per hour, and no recreational pump, healthy or not, changes that outcome. All it can do is report that the inflow exceeds capacity, and report it early.

The level sensor is the channel most vulnerable to fouling. An ultrasonic sensor above the water avoids the bilge itself, but oil mist and condensation on its face can bias its readings, and a pressure transducer at the floor can be fouled by sludge. Fouling is detected by comparing the level channel against the cycle history, and a sensor disagreement is reported as a watch item, but a fouled sensor and a failing pump can mask each other for a time, and the output says so rather than pretending certainty it does not have.

Current signatures vary by installation. Hose length, head height, and battery voltage all shift the baseline, which is why the baseline is learned per boat rather than set from a table. That learning takes two weeks and at least 30 pump cycles, and a boat that sits on the hard for the winter has no baseline until it is back in the water. A boat whose pump was just replaced has a baseline that is still learning, and its diagnoses carry lower confidence until the learning period completes.

No measurement in this system has been validated against a fleet of real boats, because the system has not been built yet. The thresholds in the claims, the 50 percent cycle-frequency rise, the 0.8-volt sag, the two-standard-deviation rule, are reasoned starting points from the physics and the published pump data, not measured results. A builder would need to instrument a few dozen boats across a season, record the failures that actually occur, and tune the thresholds against those outcomes before the confidence scores mean what they claim to mean.

## Strongest Case Against

The strongest case against this disclosure is that most of it already exists in pieces, and the pieces are close enough that a skeptic could call the combination obvious. Brunswick's 2022 application already diagnoses bilge pump malfunctions from current, including a clogged outlet, a major leak, heavy rain, a blown fuse, a failed harness, an airlock, a stalled motor, and a stuck impeller, and it runs routines to fix them and falls back to a second pump.

Brunswick's drawings also show a water-level sensor and plot current against cycle time and duration, so the distinction is not whether level is sensed at all, but whether the level is trended in millimeters against a learned baseline and turned into an inflow estimate. Blue Guard already reports pump current, voltage, and temperature over Bluetooth, and counts pump cycles. Siren Marine already alerts an owner when a pump runs too long and shows pump history and battery state. Yacht Watchman and Lash already patented remote monitoring of bilge pumps and batteries two decades ago. The new material here is the per-boat learned baseline, the inflow-rate estimation from cycle timing, the leak-versus-rain discrimination using weather data, the time-to-flooding projection from battery state, and the fleet learning. A patent examiner could reasonably say that adding a baseline and a weather feed to a current-based diagnostic is an ordinary engineering step, and that the Brunswick application plus a rain gauge gets most of the way there. That is a fair objection, and the claims are drawn to the fusion and the projection precisely because the individual pieces are not new.

The second objection is practical. A high-water alarm costs about $30, a weekly visit to the boat costs nothing but time, and the combination catches most of what this system catches, just later and with more effort. An owner who visits every weekend, lifts the float switch to test it, and looks at the battery voltage will find a stuck switch, a dead battery, and a rising leak rate, because all three are visible to a person who looks. That price is worth paying only for boats that sit unattended for weeks, for owners who cannot visit, and for leaks that grow slowly enough to hide between visits and fast enough to sink the boat before the next one. That is a real population, it includes most boats in slips during the working week, but it is not every boat, and the disclosure should not pretend otherwise.

The third objection is that current signatures are messier than the table suggests. A pump's current depends on the hose, the head, the voltage, the temperature of the water, and the age of the motor, and the ripple spectrum depends on the motor's construction in ways that vary between makers and even between production runs. A signature table with clean rows is a design document, not a measurement, and the first fleet deployment would find rows that overlap and signatures that do not separate as cleanly as written. The two-standard-deviation rule and the 14-day learning period are the honest answer to that objection, and they are also an admission that the thresholds are a starting point, not a result.

## What a Boat Owner Can Do Now

None of this requires buying anything, and the checks below take about 30 minutes on a boat in its slip. They are the manual version of what the system does automatically, and doing them once a month through the wet season covers most of the risk.

1. **Lift the float switch.** With the pump in automatic mode, lift the float by hand. The pump should start within a second. Let it go. The pump should stop. If it does not start, the switch, the fuse, or the wiring has failed. If it does not stop, the switch is stuck open and the pump is running dry.
2. **Pour water in the bilge and time the pump.** Before you run this test, look for an oil sheen on the bilge water; if oil is present, do not pump it overboard, contain it with an absorbent pad and notify the marina. Pour a measured gallon into the bilge and time how long the pump takes to clear it. A Rule 2000 at its installed head should move roughly 20 gallons a minute, so a gallon should take about three seconds of pumping. If it takes ten seconds, the strainer is clogged, the hose is kinked, or the pump is worn.
3. **Read the battery at rest and under load.** With the pump off, a full 12-volt battery reads about 12.6 volts. Start the pump and read it again. A sag of more than a volt means the battery is sulfated or the wiring is corroded. The diagnostic in this disclosure warns earlier, at 0.8 volts, because it watches the trend; a single manual reading needs the larger margin. Check the reading at the battery posts and at the pump, and a difference between the two means the wiring is the problem.
4. **Check the fuse and the connections.** Pull the bilge pump fuse and look at it. A fuse that has been hot shows discoloration. Look at every connection in the bilge. Green or white corrosion on a terminal is voltage drop waiting to happen, and a connection below the high-water mark is a connection that has been wet.
5. **Count the cycles for a week.** Write down how many times the pump runs each day and how long each run lasts. A boat that ran twice a day last month and runs six times a day now has a leak that grew, even if the bilge still looks dry between cycles. The trend is the diagnosis.
6. **Inspect the bellows and the below-waterline hoses once a year.** When the boat is hauled, flex the sterndrive bellows and look for cracks, and squeeze every below-waterline hose. A hose that feels soft or shows cracking at the clamp is a hose that will fail in the water. Replace bellows every three to five years, and replace a hose on condition, not on a schedule.
7. **Test the high-water alarm separately.** Lift the high-water float or pour water past its trigger level. The alarm should sound. An alarm that has never been tested is an alarm that may never have worked, and the day the primary pump fails is a bad day to find out.
8. **Confirm the charger.** With shore power connected, the battery should read near 13.6 volts at rest. If it reads 12.6 or lower with the cord plugged in, the charger is off, the breaker tripped, or the cord is unplugged, and the boat is running on battery alone.

Know a boat owner who has not lifted their float switch since summer? Send them this page. The checks above take about thirty minutes.
