Your cat's kidneys fail quietly. The litter box sees it first. A cat with kidney disease drinks more and pees more, often before an owner notices a change. The litter box is where that change first shows up, and nobody measures it. This disclosure measures it: a scale under the box, a microphone listening to the visit, and a gas sensor watching the air above the litter, fused into one per-cat baseline that may flag the change earlier than a yearly blood test. ## Abstract

Chronic kidney disease is a leading cause of death in cats (refs 1, 2), and it is usually found late. Creatinine, the standard blood marker, does not rise until about 75 percent of kidney function is gone (ref 1). The newer marker SDMA rises earlier, when about 40 percent is lost, but it still requires a blood draw, a vet visit, and an owner who suspected something. Cats with kidney disease use the litter box differently from healthy cats. In the kidney-disease study, cats visited more often, passed more urine per visit, spent longer eliminating, and covered less, while total time in the box was shorter because covering collapsed. In the elimination-disease study, diseased cats instead stayed shorter per visit, so the direction of the stay change depends on the disease (refs 6, 7). A 2026 study of 136 cats with a load-cell litter box monitor found cats with kidney disease urinated about twice as often, passed substantially more urine by weight, spent longer eliminating, and covered less forcefully afterward. A machine learning model separated sick from healthy cats with a weighted F1 of 92.7 percent in training and 89.9 percent in validation, though it caught only 57.1 percent of the kidney disease cats in validation, 4 of 7 validation cats with the disease (ref 7). The signal is real. The recall is not good enough, which is why this disclosure adds three more channels to the load cell.

Disclosed is a system and method for screening feline renal and urinary health from the litter box itself, using four channels that no existing product combines. A load cell under the box measures the mass of urine passed per visit, one gram standing in for about one milliliter, and weighs the cat on entry for identity and for the per-kilogram normalization the clinical thresholds require. A microphone classifies the sounds of a visit: digging, the urine stream, straining vocalization, and post-elimination covering. A metal-oxide semiconductor gas sensor tracks ammonia and total volatile organic compounds above the litter, read as change from a per-box baseline, not against an absolute threshold, because no feline litter-box ammonia threshold exists in the citable literature. A visit-pattern channel records frequency, duration, and time of day per cat, and runs a change-point detector over a learned per-cat baseline, so the system flags a change in the cat rather than a crossing of a population threshold whose value the veterinary literature has not settled.

Four channels combine into a screening score with four tiers, and a classifier maps channel patterns to renal, urinary, and behavioral explanations. The claimed combination is the load-mass-delta plus acoustic-visit-classification plus relative-VOC plus per-cat change-point fusion, not any one sensor alone and not the four-channel screening score as a whole. The system screens. It does not diagnose. Every Vet Visit alert says what veterinary test to ask for, and every Urgent alert directs the owner to emergency veterinary care now. Whether the screen flags disease earlier than bloodwork is unproven: the cited study states its system does not prove pre-diagnosis detection, and no lead time over bloodwork is claimed here.

No new sensor needs inventing. Load cells cost a few dollars, MEMS microphones are in every phone, metal-oxide gas sensors are commodity parts, and passive infrared motion sensors are in every hallway. What does not exist is the system that puts them under a litter box, learns what normal looks like for one specific cat, and says, in plain language, that this cat started peeing twice as much three weeks ago.

> Not the sensors, not the score, but the decision to compare the cat to itself.

## Field of the Invention

This invention relates to companion animal health screening: continuous, non-invasive monitoring of feline renal and urinary health from a litter box. A load-cell urine-volume proxy, acoustic visit classification, relative volatile organic compound sensing, and per-cat change-point detection fuse into a screening score. A classifier separates a chronic kidney disease pattern, a urinary straining pattern, a urethral obstruction pattern, a litter-aversion pattern, and a dehydration pattern, with a diabetes or hyperthyroid pattern reported as a competing explanation and a gas-only pattern reporting no diagnosis.

## Background

Chronic kidney disease kills cats slowly and quietly. It is a leading cause of death in cats (refs 1, 2), and by the time a cat looks sick the disease is advanced. The standard blood marker, creatinine, does not rise until about 75 percent of kidney function is gone, and SDMA, the newer marker, rises when about 40 percent is lost (ref 1). Both require a blood draw. Between vet visits, which for a healthy cat may be a year apart, nobody measures anything. The disease progresses in the gap.

The litter box shows the change. A failing kidney cannot concentrate urine, so the cat passes more dilute urine, drinks more to compensate, and visits the box more often. The 2026 iCatCare consensus guidelines record screening prevalences of 28 to 81 percent in cats over 12, with subclinical disease in 8 percent of apparently healthy older cats, the wide range reflecting different populations and screening methods (ref 2). The clinical picture is polyuria and polydipsia: too much urine, too much drinking. The veterinary primary source defines polyuria in cats as more than 40 mL/kg/day (ref 5) against a normal 18 to 28 mL/kg/day (ref 4), and polydipsia as more than 45 mL/kg/day of water intake (ref 5). But the thresholds are not settled. A looser polyuria figure of 50 mL/kg/day appears in some clinical summaries (ref 18), and the polydipsia figure in live use runs from 45 to 100 mL/kg/day depending on the source (refs 5, 19), with normal total intake about 40 to 60 mL/kg/day including water in food (ref 19). A wet-fed cat may drink only about 30 mL a day from a bowl while a dry-fed cat of the same weight drinks over 200 mL (ref 5). For a 4 kilogram cat, total intake of 160 to 240 mL a day reconciles both figures, since food water counts toward the total (ref 19), and the 45 line sits inside the normal 40 to 60 range, which is itself evidence the threshold is unsettled. Any screen built on a single absolute threshold inherits that argument. A screen built on the cat's own history does not.

The lower urinary tract has a different signature and a sharper deadline. Feline lower urinary tract disease is 1.5 to 4.5 percent of the whole feline caseload, and feline idiopathic cystitis, inflammation with no identified cause, accounts for 55 to 67 percent of it, about 0.8 to 3.0 percent of caseload in absolute terms (ref 8). Bacterial infection, the cause owners most often assume, causes only about 2 percent of lower urinary tract signs in cats under 10. The signs are pollakiuria, frequent urination of small amounts, stranguria, straining, and dysuria, painful urination, each distinct from polyuria, which is large volume.

The emergency form is urethral obstruction, mostly in male cats, with neutered males at increased risk, and an incidence of 0.54 to 3.4 percent (ref 9). A blocked cat strains, visits repeatedly, and passes little or nothing. More than 90 percent survive to discharge with treatment, but up to 85 percent arrive with azotaemia, waste products building in the blood, and the clinical consensus is that an untreated obstruction can kill within 24 to 48 hours. That consensus is stated as consensus, not as a measured median, because no survival-time trial exists. Recurrent obstruction runs about 36 percent. The difference between a cat that needs a vet visit next week and a cat that needs one tonight is written in the litter box, in visit frequency, in volume per visit, and in sound.

Owners are poor instruments for reading it. Most owners cannot say how many times their cat used the box yesterday, and clump size is judged by eye, if at all. A 2024 motion-sensor study of 37 healthy cats and 19 cats with elimination disease gives the measured baseline: healthy cats made a median of 3 visits a day, with a reference interval of 1.0 to 7.0, a median total stay of 192 seconds a day, and a mean stay of 59 seconds per elimination, while diseased cats visited significantly more often and stayed significantly less time per visit (ref 6). The study used motion sensors, not weight, and it measured visits, not volume. It shows the pattern exists and can be counted. It does not show what the urine weighed.

The closest published work is Langenfeld-McCoy and colleagues, 2026 (ref 7), a retrospective study of 136 cats using a load-cell litter box monitor with supervised machine learning over hundreds of thousands of video-labelled events. Cats with kidney disease urinated about twice as often, passed substantially more urine by weight, spent longer eliminating, and covered less forcefully afterward, while total time in the box was shorter because covering collapsed. The model reached a weighted F1, weighted by class support, of 92.7 percent in training and 89.9 percent in validation. Kidney-disease recall in validation was 57.1 percent, on 7 validation cats with the disease, and the authors state plainly that the system is not a diagnostic tool and does not prove pre-diagnosis detection. That paper is the load-cell leg of this disclosure, demonstrated. It is also the gap this disclosure addresses: load alone misses nearly half the sick cats in validation, and it says nothing about straining, about obstruction, or about what the air above the litter is doing.

Smart litter boxes already exist, and they define the boundary of what this disclosure adds. The Litter-Robot 4 weighs the cat per visit on a base scale, watches the box with laser curtain sensors, and tracks visit frequency, litter level, and waste-drawer level (ref 11). It does not measure urine volume, it has no ammonia or volatile organic compound sensor, and it has no acoustic sensor. The PETKIT Purobot Max Pro 2 adds an AI camera with facial recognition for up to 15 cats, photographs clumps after each cycle, infers urine pH from the image, and detects yowling (ref 12). Its pH is image-based, not chemical sensing. It states no load-cell urine-mass measurement and no gas sensor. Neither of the two products reviewed states an ammonia sensor, a urine-volume proxy, or acoustic classification of digging, stream, and straining. The yowling detection in the Petkit line is a commercial feature, not a published study, and yowling is not straining: a cat can strain without a sound, and a cat can yowl for other reasons.

Acoustic classification of feline litter-box sounds has no published study behind it. No peer-reviewed work on acoustic detection of cat digging, urine stream, or straining in a litter box was found in the searches behind this disclosure. The nearest analogue is livestock: an acoustic urine sensor strapped to the rear leg of dairy cattle reached F1 of 0.91 in calibration and 0.87 in validation, with a urination-duration model at R squared of 0.87 in validation (ref 17), and patents cover acoustic urination detection for livestock (ref 13) and human urination sound to flow and volume prediction (ref 14). A negative search is not proof of absence, and a patent-class search before filing is still warranted, but the feline acoustic gap is real as far as the citable literature reaches. Change-point detection has likewise not been verified as applied to feline litter-box data. The 2026 load-cell paper uses supervised classification, not change-point methods, so the per-cat change-point leg is claimed here as method, not as known practice.

Adjacent disclosures in this series cover parts of this ground. LITF-PA-2026-029 (ref 16) screens companion animal health from smartphone video of gait and behavior, a camera on the animal, not sensors in the box, and it measures movement, not elimination. No disclosure in the series measures urine volume, litter-box acoustics, or litter-box gas, and none runs a per-cat change-point detector on elimination behavior. The claimed combination is the fusion of a load-cell urine-mass proxy with acoustic visit classification, relative volatile organic compound sensing, and per-cat change-point detection, applied to feline renal and urinary screening, with the classifier that separates a chronic kidney disease pattern, a urinary straining pattern, a urethral obstruction pattern, a litter-aversion pattern, and a dehydration pattern, with a diabetes or hyperthyroid competing explanation and a gas-only pattern reporting no diagnosis.

What is missing is a litter box that knows its cat: one baseline per cat, learned over weeks, watched for the specific changes that mean kidney disease, urinary disease, or obstruction, built from commodity sensors and stated openly as a screen, not a diagnosis.

## Detailed Description

### 1. System Architecture

Two parts make up the system, a sensing node at the litter box and a fusion service that can run on a hub in the house or in the cloud. The node sits under or beside an ordinary litter box and needs no modification to the box beyond a load platform beneath it. It reports four channel scores and a screening tier, and it keeps reporting when a channel is absent, with the remaining weights renormalized to sum to 1 and the tier thresholds unchanged, since a renormalized score already sits on the same 0 to 1 scale. All numeric thresholds, weights, action levels, and timing values in this disclosure are estimates unless stated otherwise.

Inside the node, seven subsystems share one board, counting the passive infrared and radar sensors as one presence subsystem. The optional connected water fountain is separate and does not share the board. Four load cells in a platform under the box, 20 kilogram range each with 1 gram resolution, read total box mass at 10 Hz. A passive infrared sensor and a low-power radar presence sensor watch the box entrance and mark visit start and end. A digital MEMS microphone, ICS-43434 class, mounted on the box rim or the wall behind it, captures visit audio at 16 kHz. A metal-oxide semiconductor gas sensor, MQ-137 ammonia class plus a total volatile organic compound element, samples the air above the litter once per second. A temperature and humidity sensor records the air around the box, because gas sensor response drifts with humidity and the fusion corrects for it. An RFID reader at the box entrance reads collar tags where fitted, and an optional connected water fountain may report water intake to the fusion service. Powered from a wall adapter, the node draws under 3 watts and reports over Wi-Fi or Bluetooth Low Energy to the hub. No camera is used anywhere in the system, by design: the box is a private place, and the acoustic and mass channels carry the signal without a lens.

The microphone hears the house, not only the box, and the disclosure states what happens to that audio. Raw audio is processed on the node and discarded immediately after feature extraction. It is never stored and never transmitted in any deployment mode, including cloud fusion, and human speech is not retained or transcribed. The owner can mute the microphone at any time, which leaves the acoustic channel absent with the weights renormalized and the tier thresholds unchanged. The node shows a visible indicator while the microphone is live. The owner should tell everyone in the household, and guests, that a microphone is live in the room. Where a collar tag is fitted, the collar must be a breakaway collar, so it releases if it snags, and cats that will not tolerate a collar use mass and pattern attribution instead.

Identity matters, because most multi-cat homes have one box per cat plus one, and a household baseline is not a per-cat baseline. The node identifies the cat three ways, in order of reliability. First, an RFID reader at the box entrance reads a collar tag where the owner has fitted one, which is the reliable route and the recommended install, on a breakaway collar. Second, entry mass: the load platform weighs the cat as it steps in, and cats in one home usually differ by more than the 100 gram identification margin, so a stable entry mass identifies the cat where tags are absent. The 100 gram margin is an estimate, and what share of multi-cat pairs fall within it is unmeasured. Third, visit pattern: where two cats are within 100 grams and neither wears a tag, the owner is asked to fit tags, and until tags are fitted the classifier attempts attribution over entry mass, visit duration, and time-of-day habit. A visit is attributed only where the classifier is confident; a visit it cannot attribute confidently is flagged as unattributed rather than assigned to the wrong cat. A visit the system cannot attribute is counted in the household totals and excluded from every per-cat baseline. A single-cat home skips all of this.

Installation takes about ten minutes by the owner and no tools beyond a level. The platform goes under the box, the box goes back on top, the microphone clips to the rim, the gas sensor mounts on the wall behind the box at litter height, and the node plugs into the nearest outlet. On first power-up the node enters a 14 day learning period, during which it records the per-cat baseline: daily urine volume by mass, visit frequency, mean visit duration, covering duration, and the gas baseline across the litter cycle from fresh litter to change day. Learned baselines replace fixed thresholds after the learning period, because every cat has a different normal and every litter has a different smell. During the 14 day learning period no non-urgent tier is reported; the system reports a learning status, and the obstruction pattern of section 7, repeated unproductive visits, still alerts immediately, because an obstruction cannot wait two weeks for a baseline, and so does the straining pattern on absolute criteria, frequent short visits passing little urine, because cystitis cannot wait two weeks either. Absolute-value flags still fire during the learning period, such as crossing the published polyuria line, since they need no baseline; they are shown as notices, not tiers, and only non-urgent tier reporting is suppressed.

A baseline learned from a sick cat is a sick baseline, and the disclosure mitigates that failure at install rather than leaving it in Limitations alone. The owner is recommended to get a baseline vet visit with SDMA, creatinine, and urine specific gravity before or during the learning period, and the device warns at install that it cannot detect disease already present when learning starts and that it is blind during the 14 day learning period except for the obstruction pattern. Where the baseline vet visit finds disease, the learned baseline is a diseased baseline, the absolute-value flags still apply, and the owner is told the screen cannot catch what is already there. The owner should also watch for box avoidance after install: keep a plain second box available during the first weeks, so the cat always has an unmonitored option, and if the cat avoids the box once the platform, microphone, and sensor are fitted, the device is removed, and elimination outside the box is never punished.

One rule governs missing channels throughout the system. Any channel that is unavailable, a gas sensor warming up, a microphone saturated by a nearby washing machine, an RFID reader with a dead tag battery, a muted microphone, is excluded from the fusion, and the remaining channel weights are renormalized to sum to 1, with the tier thresholds unchanged. No channel ever defaults to a numeric value that silently shifts the score.

### 2. Load-Cell Urine Volume Channel

Mass is the channel that turns a guess into a number. The load platform reads total box mass continuously. A visit is marked by the presence sensor: entry when the cat steps in, exit when it steps out. The mass delta across a visit is the urine passed, plus feces where the cat defecates, minus litter kicked out, with one gram standing in for about one milliliter of urine, an approximation typically good to about 3 to 4 percent at specific gravity near 1.035, up to about 6 percent at the concentrated end of the feline range, and running lower in dilute kidney-disease urine. Feces are separated by mass and by sound: a fecal event adds 10 to 40 grams in a single step with no stream sound, an estimate, unvalidated, while urination adds mass gradually over the stream duration the acoustic channel measures, so the load channel and the acoustic channel corroborate each other on every visit. The ranges overlap, since a 4 kilogram cat passes roughly 18 to 56 grams of urine per visit against 10 to 40 grams for feces, so separation rests on the acoustic corroboration, not on mass alone. Litter kicked out is a small negative drift across the visit, corrected by the pre-visit and post-visit stable masses. Where the cat digs vigorously, the mass trace oscillates, and the visit delta is read from the stable masses before entry and after exit, not from the noisy middle.

Per-visit volume is normalized by the cat's body mass, read at entry, so the channel speaks the language of the clinical thresholds: milliliters per kilogram per day, summed across the day's visits. A 4 kilogram cat passing 120 mL in a day is at 30 mL/kg/day, above the normal 18 to 28 range (ref 4) and below the 40 polyuria line (ref 5). The channel does not claim the 40 line as a tripwire. It claims the change: the 7-day mean daily volume against the learned per-cat baseline, with a change-point detector described in section 5. The absolute line is reported alongside, as context, with the 40 versus 50 disagreement stated wherever it appears.

Scoring uses a clamp on the volume change: L = clamp((change minus 0.25) / (0.75 minus 0.25), 0, 1), where change is the fractional rise of the 7-day mean daily urine volume above the learned per-cat baseline, so a 25 percent rise scores zero and a 75 percent rise scores one. A rise that crosses the 40 mL/kg/day line adds a named flag, not extra score: the flag says the cat has crossed the published polyuria line, and the score says how far the cat has moved from its own normal. Both are shown. The load channel also carries a floor: where daily volume falls by more than 50 percent from baseline for 3 days, the channel scores on the falling side instead, because a cat that stops passing urine may be obstructed, dehydrated, or avoiding the box, and each of those needs a vet, not a shrug. The falling-side score is L_fall = clamp((0.50 minus ratio) / (0.50 minus 0.10), 0, 1), where ratio is the 3-day mean volume divided by baseline, so a ratio of 0.10 or below scores one. L is the maximum of the rising and falling scores. For the obstruction pattern, the falling formula is applied per visit rather than on a 3-day mean: a visit passing under 5 grams against the cat's normal per-visit volume scores the falling side, since an obstruction unfolds over hours, not days.

Load cells drift, and litter is the drift. Fresh litter is dry and light; used litter absorbs urine and gains mass; a full litter change drops the box mass by kilograms in a minute. The node handles this by re-taring on every litter change, detected as a mass drop of more than 500 grams while no cat is present, and by tracking the slow upward drift of used litter as background, subtracted from the visit deltas. A litter change mid-visit is impossible to miss: the cat leaves, the owner dumps the box, the mass falls, the node re-tares. Where the owner tops up litter instead of changing it, the added mass is a step while no cat is present and is likewise excluded from visit deltas. The platform is calibrated at install with a 1 kilogram test mass, passing within plus or minus 5 grams, else the platform is re-leveled or replaced.

### 3. Acoustic Visit Classification Channel

Sound is the channel that says what the cat was doing, not just that it was there. Every visit is captured at 16 kHz and classified into four event types, in order. Digging is broadband scratching noise, 1 to 4 kHz, in bursts of 0.5 to 3 seconds, usually before elimination. The urine stream is a continuous noise, 2 to 6 kHz, lasting 5 to 30 seconds, with a steady envelope, the sound of liquid hitting litter. Straining vocalization is a low-frequency cry, 300 to 900 Hz fundamental with harmonics, occurring during the elimination posture, distinct from ordinary meowing by its timing inside the visit and its repetition. Covering is broadband scratching after elimination, usually shorter and less forceful than digging, and its duration is the feature the 2026 study found tracks with kidney disease (ref 7). These frequency bands and durations are proposed, unmeasured values: no published feline data fixes them, and they are estimates to be measured on real cats.

The classifier is a small convolutional model running on the node, trained on labelled litter-box audio, with the four event types plus a silence class. Each visit produces an event sequence with timestamps: digging duration, stream duration, straining vocalization count, covering duration. From these the channel derives three features. Stream duration estimates urine volume independently of the load cell, and the two estimates are compared on every visit. Each is expressed as a fractional deviation from its own baseline, stream duration against its baseline and load mass against its baseline. Where the two deviations differ by more than 50 percentage points, the visit is flagged as discordant and both estimates are reported. A long stream with no mass delta means the sensors disagree: the microphone heard liquid and the scale saw none, so the visit is flagged rather than trusted. A mass delta with no stream means the cat passed urine silently or the microphone missed it. Straining vocalization count is one obstruction signal, and its absence is not evidence of absence, since a cat can strain without a sound. Covering duration is the renal signal: a fall in covering duration against baseline, sustained over 7 days, is the behavioral change the 2026 study measured.

Scoring uses a clamp on the largest feature deviation. A_stream = clamp((stream_rise minus 0.25) / 0.50, 0, 1) for a rise in mean stream duration against baseline. A_strain = clamp((strain_count minus 1) / 3, 0, 1) for straining vocalizations per day above one, so two a day scores 0.33 and four a day scores one. A_strain is coarse-grained, stepping in thirds with each additional vocalization per day, while the other sub-scores are continuous. A_cover = clamp((cover_fall minus 0.25) / 0.50, 0, 1) for a fall in mean covering duration against baseline. A is the maximum of the three. A straining vocalization is counted only when it occurs inside a visit, between entry and exit, so a cat crying at the box from outside does not count. The acoustic channel is confounded by the house: a washing machine, a television, a dog barking. The microphone is directional toward the box, the classifier is trained with household noise as a negative class, and a visit whose audio is dominated by non-box noise is flagged as noisy and excluded from the acoustic features, though the visit itself still counts in the load and frequency channels.

Nothing validates acoustic classification in cats yet, stated plainly because the rest of this section depends on it. The livestock analogue reaches F1 of 0.91 in calibration and 0.87 in validation for cattle urination (ref 17), which shows the signal class is learnable in animals, not that it is learned in cats. The acoustic features in this disclosure are estimates that need measurement on real cats in real homes, and the acoustic channel never opens the Urgent tier alone. An obstruction call requires the load and frequency pattern of section 7. That means four or more visits in each of two consecutive hours, each with a mass delta below 5 grams, with straining vocalization as corroboration and not as a requirement. Or it means no urine passed in 12 hours with repeated visits. Or it means four or more unproductive visits, each passing under 5 grams, in 2 hours followed by no visits at all for the next 6 hours.

### 4. Volatile Organic Compound and Ammonia Channel

Gas is the weakest channel and the disclosure says so. A metal-oxide semiconductor sensor above the litter reads ammonia and total volatile organic compounds once per second. Cat urine contains urea, which bacteria convert to ammonia as litter ages, and the smell of a box rises over the days between litter changes. The channel measures two things: the spike after each visit, as fresh urine adds ammonia to the headspace, and the slow baseline rise across the litter cycle, as the litter loads. The spike size tracks the volume passed, weakly, and the baseline rise tracks litter age, strongly. Neither is a clinical number.

The reason the channel is relative is that no absolute number exists to compare against. The OSHA 8-hour ammonia limit is 50 ppm and the NIOSH recommended limit is 25 ppm, with a short-term limit of 35 ppm and an immediately dangerous level of 300 ppm (ref 10), but those are worker-exposure limits, not feline health thresholds. No published feline litter-box ammonia level and no feline safe threshold was found in the citable literature, and figures circulating in product marketing that claim specific litter-box parts-per-million values trace to unverifiable sources and are not cited here. The disclosure therefore claims no ppm threshold. It claims the relative pattern. The post-visit spike divided by the visit volume the load cell measured gives a spike per milliliter that tracks urine concentration, rising against the cat's own history, which is consistent with more concentrated urine. The baseline ammonia across the litter cycle rising faster than the litter-age model predicts is consistent with a change in urine composition or volume.

Scoring uses a clamp on the relative spike: V = clamp((spike_ratio minus 1.25) / (2.0 minus 1.25), 0, 1), where spike_ratio is the mean post-visit ammonia spike per milliliter over 7 days divided by the learned per-cat baseline spike per milliliter, corrected for litter age and humidity. A 25 percent rise scores zero and a doubling scores one. The gas sensor is corrected for humidity from the temperature and humidity sensor on the node, and the litter-age model is a learned curve of baseline ammonia against days since litter change, fitted per household. A litter change resets the model. The gas channel is weighted lowest in the fusion, 0.15, because it is the least specific: diet, litter brand, and room ventilation all move it, and the sensor drifts with age. It corroborates. It never leads.

### 5. Visit Pattern and Change-Point Detection

Frequency is the channel the 2026 study validated, and the change-point detector is the method that makes it per-cat. Per cat per day, the visit-pattern channel records visit count, mean visit duration, mean stay per elimination, and the time-of-day distribution. Mean stay per elimination means time in the box per elimination visit, as the motion-sensor study measured it, not the voiding duration alone: in the kidney-disease study the voiding duration was longer while total time in the box was shorter, because covering collapsed. Baselines come from the 14-day learning-period median for each quantity, and the detector is a cumulative sum, CUSUM, over daily values, run in both directions. The rising side is S_t = max(0, S_{t-1} + (x_t minus baseline minus k)), and the falling side is a mirror, S-_t = max(0, S-_{t-1} + (baseline minus k minus x_t)), so falling patterns, dehydration and obstruction, get a change-point date just as rising patterns do. The tuning constant k is set to half the shift the system is tuned to detect: 0.5 visits a day for frequency, 15 percent of baseline for volume, and 15 percent of baseline for covering duration, all estimates. When either sum crosses the alarm threshold h, set to 5 times the daily standard deviation of the baseline period, the detector fires, and the fire date is the change point. The detector runs separately on frequency, volume, and covering duration, and the earliest fire among them is the reported change point. A fire requires the shift to persist: a single bad day moves the sum but does not cross h, and the sum resets to zero when the daily value returns below baseline plus k on the rising side or above baseline minus k on the falling side.

The cat's own baseline is what makes this channel defensible where absolute thresholds are not. The polydipsia threshold in live use runs from above 45 (ref 5) to above 100 mL/kg/day (ref 19), and the polyuria line is argued between 40 (ref 5) and 50 (ref 18). A cat whose normal is 20 mL/kg/day and rises to 35 has changed by 75 percent and is still below every published line, and a screen built on the line would miss it. A cat whose normal is 38 and rises to 42 has crossed the 40 line on a 10 percent change, and a screen built on the line would flag noise. The change-point detector flags the first cat and not the second, and reports the absolute value alongside, so the vet sees both the change and the number. That is the method contribution of this disclosure: not the sensors, not the score, but the decision to compare the cat to itself.

Scoring uses a clamp on the frequency change: F = clamp((freq_rise minus 0.25) / (0.75 minus 0.25), 0, 1), where freq_rise is the fractional rise of the 7-day mean visit frequency above baseline. A second term covers the diseased pattern the 2024 study measured, more visits with shorter stays: F_short = clamp((stay_fall minus 0.25) / 0.50, 0, 1), where stay_fall is the fractional fall in mean stay per elimination against baseline. F is the maximum of the two. The change-point fire and the score are independent: the score is a magnitude, the fire is a timing, and the alert carries both. In the Early kidney row of section 6, for example, the 7-day mean frequency sits about 53 percent above baseline and daily volume about 55 percent above it, and the change-point fire dates that rise, so the owner learns not only that visits rose but when the rise began.

### 6. Fusion Score and Tiers

Four channel scores combine into one screening score. H = 0.35 L + 0.25 A + 0.15 V + 0.25 F, where L is the load score, A the acoustic score, V the gas score, and F the visit-pattern score. Weights reflect evidence strength: load and frequency are the channels with published feline data, acoustics has livestock analogue only, and gas is the least specific. No sensitivity analysis of the screening score to these weights has been performed, and the weights are estimates. A small weight change can move a borderline case across a threshold, which is why the tier boundaries are stated as ranges with a persistence requirement rather than as hard lines.

Where a channel is absent, the remaining weights are renormalized to sum to 1, and the tier thresholds are unchanged, because a renormalized score already sits on the same 0 to 1 scale. A renormalized score is not the same evidence as a four-channel score, and the alert says which channels were present.

Four tiers. No change detected is H below 0.30, meaning the cat is within its normal range; the weekly report says so. Watch is H from 0.30 to below 0.55, meaning a small change is present and worth a note at the next vet visit. A Watch tier escalates mid-week if the score crosses into the Vet Visit band, rather than waiting for the weekly report. Vet Visit is H from 0.55 to below 0.75, meaning the owner is told to book a vet visit within the week, or same-day where straining is present, and the alert names the test to request. Urgent is H at or above 0.75, and it is a single rule with two conditions. A score at or above 0.75 reaches Urgent only where the obstruction pattern of section 7 is present, or where the cat has passed no urine in 12 hours. Every other score at or above 0.75 is held at Vet Visit, and the alert says the score is high but the pattern is not the emergency pattern. A persistence rule guards the tiers. Watch requires the score in band for 3 consecutive days, Vet Visit for 2 consecutive days, and Urgent fires immediately, since an obstruction cannot wait for persistence. A single-day spike from a hot day or a litter change does not reach a tier. The tiers run on the same data, the same baseline, and the same detection math in both the health screen and the Urgent obstruction detector. There is no separate emergency subsystem. The Urgent obstruction condition is a pattern rule on top of the same fused score.

Worked example, estimates, not results:

| Cat | L | A | V | F | H | Tier |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Healthy | 0.00 | 0.00 | 0.00 | 0.20 | 0.05 | No change detected |
| Summer heat | 0.45 | 0.75 | 0.60 | 0.55 | 0.49 | Watch |
| Established kidney disease | 0.90 | 0.75 | 0.50 | 0.90 | 0.77 | Vet Visit |
| Straining, cystitis | 0.05 | 0.67 | 0.05 | 0.80 | 0.43 | Vet Visit |
| Obstruction, 2 hours in | 1.00 | 0.90 | 0.30 | 1.00 | 0.85 | Urgent |
| Dehydration | 0.30 | 0.10 | 0.00 | 0.20 | 0.16 | No change detected |
| Aversion, new litter | 0.20 | 0.20 | 0.20 | 0.05 | 0.11 | No change detected |
| Summer heat, volume only | 0.20 | 0.30 | 0.20 | 0.65 | 0.35 | Watch |

The worked example needs reading, because the table alone misleads. The Healthy row shows a frequency score of 0.20 on a cat with no change, because daily visit count varies and the clamp scores small rises above zero. The Summer heat row shows the fusion working as intended and also shows its limit: the cat drank more in a heat wave, volume rose, frequency rose, the gas channel rose as more urine sat in the litter, and the score reached Watch at 0.49. That is a false alarm in the sense that the cat is healthy, and it is a true alarm in the sense that the cat's elimination really did change. The tier says Watch, not Vet Visit, and the alert names the competing explanation, heat, so the owner can check the weather before booking a vet. The Established kidney disease row shows the screen at its best: every channel moved, the score reached 0.77, and the tier is Vet Visit, not Urgent, because the pattern is chronic, not obstructive. The Straining row shows the score at its weakest: the load channel barely moved, since the cat passes small amounts often, the gas channel barely moved, and the score sat at 0.43, in Watch. The tier is Vet Visit anyway, because the straining pattern of section 7 overrides the score. That override is the most important rule in this disclosure, and it is stated here because the table hides it: a straining cat is a Vet Visit, and an obstructed cat is Urgent, whatever the score says. The Obstruction row shows the fused score at 0.85 with the Urgent tier, because the obstruction pattern is present. The Dehydration row shows the falling side of the load channel scoring 0.30 while the other channels stay quiet, and the total at 0.16, below Watch. The Aversion row shows the same shape: small moves everywhere, total 0.11, below Watch. The last row shows a hot-weather cat whose volume rose about a third, frequency rose, and the score reached Watch at 0.35 on the frequency channel alone. The table is a worked example of the arithmetic, not a result. No cat in it is real, no false-positive or false-negative rate follows from it, and the straining row exists to show why the score is not the decision.

### 7. Classification and Differential Diagnosis

The classifier maps channel patterns to explanations, because a score without a cause is a number without advice. Each row of the table is a pattern, and the system reports the row that matches, or reports that no row matches. Seven rows, one for each explanation the literature supports, plus a competing-explanation row and a no-diagnosis row. The rows are estimated from published values and proposed thresholds, not measured on a labeled dataset, and no confusion matrix exists for any of them. Each row states its rule, and each row's confidence is Low unless stated otherwise, because no row has a measured accuracy behind it.

| Pattern | Signature | Action | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chronic kidney disease | Frequency and volume rising together for 7 or more days; covering falling | Vet Visit within the week; ask for SDMA, creatinine, urine specific gravity | Medium |
| Urinary straining, cystitis or UTI | Frequent short unproductive visits; straining vocalization or silent straining | Vet Visit same day; ask for urinalysis and culture | Medium |
| Urethral obstruction | Four or more visits in each of two consecutive hours, each passing under 5 grams, or no urine in 12 hours with repeated visits, or four or more unproductive visits in 2 hours followed by no visits for 6 hours | Urgent: go to emergency veterinary care now | High |
| Litter aversion | Visits to the box falling; elimination outside the box reported by the owner; no volume or frequency rise | Watch; try a different litter or box location | Low |
| Dehydration | Volume falling, frequency falling or flat, no straining signature | Vet Visit within the week; ask for hydration assessment | Low |
| Diabetes or hyperthyroid, competing explanation | Volume and frequency rising together with weight falling, or with water intake rising faster than urine volume | Vet Visit; mention diabetes and hyperthyroid to the vet | Low |
| Gas only, no diagnosis | Gas channel rising alone, all other channels quiet | No diagnosis; check litter age and ventilation | Low |

The kidney-disease row is the row with the most evidence, and it is still only Medium confidence. The 2026 study gives it a load-cell foundation, but the acoustic and gas legs are unvalidated, and the row requires 7 or more days of joint rise, which means it is late by design. The straining row is Medium because the pattern is specific: frequent short visits passing little urine is not a normal pattern, and the 2024 study measured the short-stay side of it. The obstruction row is High confidence because the pattern is unmistakable: a cat that visits every few minutes and passes nothing is obstructed until proven otherwise, and the cost of waiting exceeds the cost of a false alarm. The aversion row is Low because it depends on the owner reporting elimination outside the box, which the system cannot see, and because a cat that avoids the box may be avoiding the device, not the litter, so the alert says so. The dehydration row is Low because a falling volume with no straining signature has several causes, and the system cannot separate them. The diabetes or hyperthyroid row is a competing explanation, not a diagnosis: polyuria with weight loss or with water intake rising faster than urine volume points at endocrine disease, and the alert tells the owner to mention both to the vet, because kidney disease is not the only cause of drinking more. Where the water fountain is connected, a rising intake with flat urine volume suggests the cat is drinking more without passing more, which points away from kidney disease and toward diabetes or hyperthyroid, and where the fountain is not connected, the row is not available. The gas-only row reports no diagnosis, because a gas rise alone has too many causes to name one.

Straining is the row where the score is most dangerous to trust. A straining cat can obstruct while waiting, so the straining pattern overrides the score, and the override is stated twice in this disclosure because it is the rule a builder is most likely to get wrong. Where the straining pattern is present, the tier is Vet Visit same day, and the alert names urinalysis and culture as the tests to request. Where the obstruction pattern is present, the tier is Urgent, and the alert directs the owner to emergency veterinary care now. The straining pattern is present where any of three prongs fires. First, frequent short unproductive visits: four or more visits in a day, each passing under 5 grams, with mean visit duration falling, and the persistence requirement does not apply, because straining is not a trend to confirm over days. Second, vocalized straining: straining vocalization on two or more visits in a day. Third, silent straining: frequent short visits, F_short at or above 0.55, with load falling, L below 0.30, and acoustic straining sub-score at or above 0.30, or with per-visit mass down 50 percent or more from baseline. Silent straining is the prong with the weakest evidence, and the alert says so. Where the score is low and the straining pattern is present, the alert says the score is low and the pattern is what matters. Where the score is high and no pattern matches, the alert says the score is high and no cause is identified, and the tier stands.

### 8. Data Retention, Privacy, and Alerting

The system keeps what it needs and discards the rest. Per-visit features, visit times and volumes, channel scores, and tier history are kept for 90 days, and tier history is kept for one year, then deleted within 30 days of expiry. The baseline itself is kept for the life of the install. Raw audio is discarded immediately after feature extraction, on the node, in every deployment mode, and is never stored or transmitted. No video is captured. No human speech is retained or transcribed. Where the owner opts in, de-identified per-cat features, not raw audio and not visit times, may be pooled for research, with the owner able to withdraw at any time, and withdrawal deletes the pooled features. Visit times and volumes are never pooled, even with opt-in, because they reveal when the house is occupied. The owner can delete all stored data at any time, and deletion completes within 30 days.

Alerts go to the owner's phone as a notification and as a weekly summary, and the weekly summary always includes the change-point date where the detector has fired. A Vet Visit alert names the test to request: SDMA, creatinine, and urine specific gravity for the kidney row; urinalysis and culture for the straining row; hydration assessment for the dehydration row. An Urgent alert is a push notification and a text message, and it repeats every 30 minutes until acknowledged. The alert text for Urgent says the pattern and the action, not the score: "Your cat has visited the box 6 times in the last hour and passed almost no urine. Go to emergency veterinary care now." The alert does not say "H is 0.85". A score is a number for the system. A cat that cannot pass urine is an emergency for the owner.

Where a household has more than one cat and the system cannot attribute a visit, the alert says so. "An unidentified cat visited the box 5 times today and passed little urine" is an honest alert. "Your cat visited 5 times" when the system does not know which cat is a false one. Attribution confidence is reported with every per-cat alert, and an alert built on an unattributed visit is marked as such.

## Implementation Notes

Build the retrofit kit first, because it is the cheapest way to test the idea. A 20 kilogram load-cell platform under an existing box, a MEMS microphone on the rim, a metal-oxide gas sensor on the wall, and a passive infrared sensor at the entrance, reporting to a phone app. The load platform is the channel with published feline data, so build it first and validate the mass delta against weighed clumps for 30 days before adding acoustics. The microphone is the channel with the most work, since it needs a labelled dataset of litter-box audio that does not exist yet: record 100 visits across 10 cats, label digging, stream, straining vocalization, and covering by hand, and train the classifier before trusting any acoustic score. The gas sensor is the channel to add last, because it is the least specific and the easiest to misread.

The hub runs the fusion, the change-point detector, and the classifier, and it stores the per-cat baselines. A cloud service is optional, not required: the whole pipeline runs on a hub in the house, and the cloud adds only remote alerting and pooled research data. Where the cloud is used, the raw audio still never leaves the node. The owner should be able to run the system with no cloud account at all, and the alerting degrades gracefully to local notifications on the home network.

Cost is an estimate, not a quote. The retrofit kit is 40 to 80 dollars in parts at single-unit quantities: load cells and amplifier about 15 dollars, microphone about 5 dollars, gas sensor about 8 dollars, presence sensors about 5 dollars, processor and Wi-Fi about 10 dollars, enclosure and cabling the rest. The test the screen prompts, a senior blood panel with SDMA, creatinine, and urine specific gravity, costs more than the kit, and that is the point: the kit is cheap, the test is the thing that saves the cat, and the kit exists to make the owner order the test.

## Limitations

The strongest case against this approach is that the cheapest screen already exists and costs nothing: count the visits, weigh the clumps, call the vet. A kitchen scale, a notebook, and a week of attention catch the same doubling of urine volume that a 40 to 80 dollar kit catches, and the owner who will not count visits will not install a load platform either. The 2026 load-cell study caught only 57.1 percent of kidney-disease cats in validation, on 7 validation cats with the disease, so the best measured version of the load channel alone misses nearly half the sick cats it is meant to find. The acoustic channel has no feline validation at all, the gas channel has no clinical threshold to compare against, and the fusion score has no measured false-positive or false-negative rate for any tier. On that case, the four-channel system is a gadget built around a notebook problem, and the money and attention would be better spent on twice-yearly bloodwork with SDMA, which is the validated screen.

That case has real force, and the disclosure answers it in three parts, with two concessions. First, the conceded parts: the lead time over bloodwork is unproven, and no false-alarm rate is known for any tier, so the screen may flag healthy cats in summer and miss sick cats in winter, and the disclosure cannot say how often. Second, the notebook screen works only where the owner counts, and owners do not count: the 2024 study exists precisely because owner report is unreliable, and a system that counts every visit for a year catches the slow rise a notebook misses when the owner stops writing after a week. Third, the kit's value is not the sensor but the alert: a push notification that says visits doubled three weeks ago is a different intervention from a notebook the owner never opens, and the alert names the test to request, which is the step between noticing and acting.

Eight limitations are numbered below, and the paragraphs after them state further limits on the score, the weights, and the tier error rates.

First, the acoustic channel is unvalidated in cats. No published study classifies feline litter-box sounds, the frequency bands and durations in section 3 are proposed estimates, and the livestock analogue proves the signal class is learnable, not that it is learned in cats. A classifier trained on 10 cats in one home may fail on a 20th cat in another home, and the disclosure has no data on breed, age, or litter-type variation in the acoustic features.

Second, the gas channel has no clinical threshold. No feline litter-box ammonia level and no feline safe threshold was found, so the channel is relative by necessity, and a relative rise in ammonia per milliliter may reflect diet, litter brand, or ventilation rather than urine concentration. The gas sensor drifts with age and humidity, and the litter-age model is a learned curve that has never been fitted on a real household.

Third, the load channel separates urine from feces by mass and sound, and the ranges overlap. A 4 kilogram cat passes roughly 18 to 56 grams of urine per visit against 10 to 40 grams for feces, so a visit with no stream sound and a 25 gram delta is ambiguous, and the disclosure's answer is corroboration, not resolution. Where the microphone is muted or the audio is noisy, the separation rests on mass alone, and it is weak.

Fourth, the fusion weights are estimates with no sensitivity analysis. No sensitivity analysis of the screening score to the weights or thresholds has been performed, so a small change in a weight can move a tier-boundary case across a threshold. The table in section 6 is a worked example, not a result, and no false-positive or false-negative rate is known for any tier.

Fifth, the change-point detector has never run on feline litter-box data. The CUSUM tuning constants and the alarm threshold of five standard deviations are estimates, and the detector's false-alarm rate on real cats is unknown. A cat whose routine changes seasonally, a new housemate, a move, a diet change, will fire the detector, and the disclosure relies on the classifier and the owner to separate those from disease.

Sixth, the baseline is learned from a cat that may already be sick. A baseline vet visit is recommended at install, but it is a recommendation, not an enforcement, and a cat that starts the learning period with established kidney disease learns a diseased baseline. The absolute-value flags still apply, but the change-point detector is blind to disease already present when learning starts, and the disclosure cannot detect what it never saw change.

Seventh, multi-cat attribution is the weakest link in the system. Where two cats are within 100 grams and neither wears a tag, attribution rests on pattern, and a visit assigned to the wrong cat corrupts two baselines at once. The unattributed-visit flag mitigates this by excluding uncertain visits, but it also means a household with two similar cats and no tags may exclude a large share of visits from both baselines.

Eighth, the system depends on adoption by people who do not think about their litter box. A monitor that needs an owner to install a platform, fit a breakaway collar, and read its notices will sit uninstalled in exactly the homes where a cat is quietly getting sicker, and the disclosure's cheapest competitor, a notebook, has the same problem.

Two further limits are stated in the body and repeated here. The straining pattern's silent prong, F_short at or above 0.55 with load falling and acoustic straining sub-score at or above 0.30, or per-visit mass down 50 percent or more, is the prong with the weakest evidence, and the alert says so. The cessation prong of the obstruction pattern, four or more unproductive visits in 2 hours followed by no visits for 6 hours, fires late, about 8 hours after the obstruction begins, but inside the 24 to 48 hour window, and the disclosure states that delay plainly. No lead time over bloodwork is claimed anywhere in this disclosure, and the 2026 study's authors state their system does not prove pre-diagnosis detection.

**Do this today:** Count your cat's litter box visits for one week. Weigh the clumps if you use clumping litter, with gloves, and wash your hands after handling litter. If visits double over days, or clumps get much bigger, call your vet the same day. For any cat over 10, ask for twice-yearly bloodwork with SDMA and a urine specific gravity. If your cat strains, cries in the box, or visits every few minutes and passes almost nothing, go to emergency veterinary care now.
